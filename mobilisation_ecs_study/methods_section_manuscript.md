# 2. Methods

## 2.1 Research design and methodological orientation

This study used Interpretive Description, a qualitative methodology developed within nursing to generate contextually grounded knowledge for clinical practice (Thorne, 2025). The approach suited the research question for three reasons. Mobilisation decisions after emergency caesarean birth are a practice problem, made within routine postnatal care and shaped by clinical and organisational circumstances. Interpretive Description also asks the analyst to move beyond describing experience towards interpretations that clinicians can recognise and use. Finally, it allows existing disciplinary knowledge to frame the inquiry as a provisional scaffold rather than a theory to be confirmed (Thorne, 2025).

The study adopted a constructivist–interpretive position. Participants' accounts were treated as situated explanations of how decisions were reasoned about, communicated and revised, rather than as objective records of bedside events. The analysis therefore interpreted reported decision-making and did not evaluate the clinical appropriateness of individual encounters.

Relational autonomy (Entwistle et al., 2010; Stoljar and Mackenzie, 2023) and the orientation presented in Figure 1 (Section 1.1) served as sensitising concepts. They informed areas of inquiry in the interview guides, including the information women received, opportunities to express preferences, requests for assistance or reassessment, and the constraints within which participation occurred. They did not supply a coding frame, predefined categories or expected findings. Codes were developed inductively, and the distinctions in Figure 1 were examined against the data during analysis rather than assumed (Section 2.6).

The phenomenon of interest was participants' reported decisions about first assisted mobilisation and subsequent progression during the initial postoperative hospital admission. The activities were changing position in bed, sitting on the edge of the bed, transferring out of bed, standing and walking. Consistent with the Introduction, readiness appraisals were defined as judgements about a woman's current capacity, clinically assessed and perceived safety, willingness and support requirements for a specified activity. Decisions were defined as reported actions to attempt, assist, adapt, pause or defer that activity, and reconsideration as any reported revision of an earlier appraisal or decision. Mobilisation after discharge was outside the scope of the study unless participants related it to in-hospital decisions.

The study is reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ; Tong et al., 2007), which apply to the whole report. The completed checklist, with manuscript locations for each item, is provided in Supplementary File S2.

## 2.2 Study setting and mobilisation care pathways

### 2.2.1 National context

In Saudi Arabia, public healthcare is being reorganised into regional health clusters under the Health Holding Company as part of the Health Sector Transformation Program (Health Holding Company, n.d.). Private hospitals operate alongside the cluster system under Ministry of Health licensing. Nurses, midwives and physiotherapists are separately classified and registered by the Saudi Commission for Health Specialties (SCFHS). In the participating hospitals, as in many Saudi hospitals, midwives practised mainly in labour and delivery units, whereas postnatal wards were staffed by registered nurses working in maternity care. Maternity nurses were therefore the professionals responsible for routine postoperative mobilisation support, and midwives were not recruited as a separate group.

### 2.2.2 Participating hospitals and pathways

The study was conducted in Al-Jouf Region, northern Saudi Arabia. Hospitals A and B were government hospitals within Al-Jouf Health Cluster, and Hospital C was a private regional hospital outside the cluster. Each hospital provided emergency obstetric surgery and had a dedicated postnatal ward. After emergency caesarean birth, women were transferred from the operating theatre to a post-anaesthesia care unit and, once anaesthetic discharge criteria were met, to the postnatal ward, where first assisted mobilisation and subsequent progression took place. Women requiring high-dependency or intensive care were outside the study.

The three hospitals shared a common basic pathway. Before first assisted mobilisation, a maternity nurse assessed the woman's vital signs, pain, sensory and motor recovery after neuraxial anaesthesia, dizziness and bleeding, and the presence of urinary catheters, drains and infusions. The nurse then initiated and assisted mobilisation, usually in stages from sitting to standing and walking, and adjusted later assistance according to the woman's symptoms and support needs. Nurses reassessed women each shift and whenever new or worsening symptoms were reported. Physiotherapy was referral-based. When nurses identified rehabilitation needs beyond routine assistance, such as persistent inability to transfer, marked fear of movement or a pre-existing musculoskeletal condition, they reported these to the obstetric team, which initiated the referral. Physiotherapists assessed functional movement, taught transfer and walking strategies, and returned recommendations to the maternity team through the medical record and verbal handover to the nurse in charge.

Hospitals A and B operated within the overarching cluster framework, with ward-level procedures documented separately at each site. Hospital C followed its own hospital pathway. Table 1 compares the sites on the assistance role, referral criteria, referral initiator, physiotherapy availability and reassessment arrangements. The most consequential difference concerned physiotherapy availability outside routine hours. At Hospitals A and B, physiotherapists received postnatal referrals on weekday day shifts, and referrals made in the evening, at night or at weekends were seen on the next working day. Hospital C provided physiotherapy six days a week, with telephone advice outside those hours. At all sites, nurses remained responsible for mobilisation assistance when physiotherapy was unavailable.

Pathway descriptions were compiled from written ward policies and mobilisation or enhanced recovery protocols supplied by each hospital, from a structured site-description form completed with each postnatal ward's nurse manager and the physiotherapy department lead before data collection, and from field notes recorded during recruitment visits. Where written protocols and reported practice differed, both were recorded, and the analysis relied on participants' accounts of practice.

## 2.3 Participants, eligibility, sampling and recruitment

### 2.3.1 Definition of emergency caesarean birth

Emergency caesarean birth was defined as any caesarean birth that had not been scheduled in advance, decided upon after the onset of labour or because of maternal or fetal compromise, corresponding to urgency categories 1 to 3 of the classification by Lucas et al. (2000). Planned (category 4) caesarean births were excluded, including planned procedures brought forward by spontaneous labour. Urgency category was confirmed from the operative record.

### 2.3.2 Eligibility criteria

Women were eligible if they were aged 18 years or older, had given birth by emergency caesarean under neuraxial or general anaesthesia at a participating hospital, had experienced at least one assisted mobilisation episode during the initial postoperative admission, and could be interviewed in Arabic or English. Women were excluded if they or their infant were critically unwell, including maternal intensive care admission or neonatal death or critical illness, because an interview would impose an unacceptable burden during acute distress, or if they lacked capacity to consent. Women whose mobilisation had been delayed, declined, paused or deferred were deliberately retained, because such decisions are central to the research question. Fitness to be interviewed was assessed separately from readiness to mobilise: a woman unable or unwilling to walk could be entirely fit to describe how that decision was reached. Before each interview, the woman's nurse confirmed that she was alert, sufficiently comfortable and not recently sedated, and the woman confirmed that she felt able to talk.

Maternity nurses were eligible if they held current SCFHS registration, had at least six months' experience on a postnatal ward at a participating hospital, and had personally assessed or assisted mobilisation after emergency caesarean birth within the preceding three months. Nurses in supervisory roles were eligible if they continued to provide direct care. Students and agency staff were excluded because they lacked sustained involvement in the ward's mobilisation practice.

Physiotherapists were eligible if they held current SCFHS registration and had assessed or treated at least one woman after caesarean birth at a participating hospital within the preceding six months. No minimum length of experience was required, because few physiotherapists received postnatal referrals.

### 2.3.3 Sampling strategy

Purposive sampling for maximum variation was used within each group (Palinkas et al., 2015). Among women, variation was sought in hospital, parity, urgency category, type of anaesthesia, whether labour preceded the caesarean, timing of first mobilisation (within or beyond 24 hours), whether progression was straightforward or involved pausing, deferral or physiotherapy referral, and whether the infant was rooming-in or in the neonatal unit. Among nurses, variation was sought in hospital, years of postnatal experience, shift pattern, and nationality and first language, reflecting the internationally recruited nursing workforce. Among physiotherapists, variation was sought in hospital, years of experience and whether women's health formed a regular part of their caseload. As analysis progressed, recruitment was directed towards under-represented episode types, particularly women who had declined or deferred mobilisation and professionals with experience of referral.

### 2.3.4 Recruitment

At each hospital, the postnatal ward nurse manager acted as gatekeeper for women and nurses, and the physiotherapy department lead for physiotherapists. Gatekeepers circulated study information but did not approach individuals or learn who took part. For women, the nurse in charge identified potentially eligible women from the ward census and asked whether they were willing to hear about the study. With permission, the interviewing researcher (MAA) provided verbal and written information in Arabic or English and allowed at least 12 hours before seeking consent. Nurses and physiotherapists received an information sheet through ward and departmental communication channels and contacted the researchers directly if interested. Neither researcher held a clinical, managerial or supervisory role at any participating hospital, professionals were not recruited through requests from line managers, and women's care providers were not told whether women had agreed to participate. All participants were assured verbally and in writing that declining would not affect their care or employment.

### 2.3.5 Linked and independent accounts

Accounts were described independently rather than linked to shared encounters. Women, nurses and physiotherapists were recruited separately, and no attempt was made to recruit the professionals involved in a particular woman's care. Professionals described mobilisation episodes from their own practice, including specific de-identified examples. This design protected confidentiality on small wards and avoided placing women and their care providers in an evaluative relationship. Cross-group comparisons therefore illuminate contrasting or converging patterns of reasoning, not corroboration of shared encounters (Section 2.6).

### 2.3.6 Sample size and adequacy

Interpretive Description does not prescribe a sample size; adequacy depends on the analytic purpose and the variation needed to support credible, clinically useful interpretation (Thorne, 2025). Planned ranges were informed by empirical evidence and by the concept of information power. A systematic review of empirical saturation studies found that 9 to 17 interviews generally reached saturation in relatively homogeneous populations with focused objectives (Hennink and Kaiser, 2022). Code saturation, in which the range of issues is identified, has been reached at around 9 interviews, whereas meaning saturation, in which issues are richly understood, required 16 to 24 (Hennink et al., 2017). Information power holds that fewer participants are needed when the aim is narrow, the sample is specific, established theory is used, dialogue is strong and analysis is case-oriented (Malterud et al., 2016).

On this basis, 16 to 20 women were planned, because women's accounts carried the greatest variation in clinical course, symptoms and newborn-care demands and needed to represent all three hospitals and episodes of delayed and deferred mobilisation, which required depth consistent with meaning saturation. Between 10 and 14 maternity nurses were planned, because nurses formed a more homogeneous group with a focused role and each could draw on many mobilisation episodes. Between 6 and 8 physiotherapists were planned, because the eligible population was small but each participant held specialised and highly specific information, provided all three hospitals were represented.

Saturation was not used as a stopping rule, because it fits interpretive analysis poorly (Braun and Clarke, 2021). Data collection ended when the research team judged, in documented analytic meetings, that the data contained sufficient concrete decision episodes across activities and recovery stages to interpret how appraisals informed decisions and their reconsideration; that each emerging interpretation was supported by accounts from more than one hospital and, where relevant, more than one participant group; and that recent interviews were refining existing interpretations rather than raising new aspects of decision reasoning.

### 2.3.7 Recruitment outcomes

The final sample comprised 37 participants: 18 women, 12 maternity nurses and 7 physiotherapists (Table 2). Of 27 women approached, 18 were interviewed. Four declined because of fatigue or a wish to focus on their infant, two declined after discussion with family, and three were discharged before an interview could be arranged and did not wish to continue after discharge. Of 16 nurses who expressed interest, 12 were interviewed; four could not schedule an interview because of shift commitments. Of 9 eligible physiotherapists, 8 expressed interest and 7 were interviewed; one withdrew before interview because of workload. Participant characteristics are reported in aggregate (Table 3) to prevent identification within small professional groups.

## 2.4 Data collection and interview guide development

Data were collected between March and September 2026 through individual semi-structured interviews. Women were offered an interview in a private room on the postnatal ward from postoperative day 2, once fitness to be interviewed had been confirmed and after at least one assisted mobilisation, or a telephone or video interview within 14 days of birth. Fourteen women were interviewed in hospital and four by telephone after discharge. In-hospital interviews were scheduled around feeding, observations and visiting. Professionals chose either a face-to-face interview in a private room away from the clinical area outside working hours or a video interview. Interviews were conducted in Arabic or English according to participant preference. Interviews with women lasted 28 to 55 minutes (median 39) and those with professionals 41 to 74 minutes (median 52).

MAA conducted all interviews with women. To reduce the influence of shared professional assumptions, interviews with professionals were allocated across disciplines: SOA, a physiotherapist, interviewed the maternity nurses, and MAA, a nurse, interviewed the physiotherapists. Both researchers are women, which was culturally appropriate for interviewing postnatal women in this setting. Their backgrounds are described in Section 2.7.1.

Three group-specific interview guides were developed from the study objectives, the sensitising concepts (Section 1.1), literature on mobilisation after caesarean birth and negotiated mobilisation (Mørch et al., 2019; Lehmkuhl et al., 2024; Thörn et al., 2025), and advice from the patient and public involvement group (Section 2.9). The guides shared a common structure to support comparison across groups but were adapted to each perspective. The women's guide emphasised bodily experience, information received, how preferences were expressed and the demands of newborn care. The nurses' guide addressed assessment, assisting and adapting movement, workload, and reporting needs to the obstetric team. The physiotherapists' guide addressed referral, functional assessment and the communication and uptake of recommendations. The guides were written in English, translated into Arabic by MAA and checked for conceptual equivalence by SOA. Colloquial Arabic expressions such as "getting up", "sitting on the edge of the bed" and "taking your first steps" replaced the technical term for mobilisation.

The guides were piloted with one woman and one maternity nurse who met the eligibility criteria. Piloting led to two changes: the women's opening question was moved from a general question about recovery to a concrete question about the first time they got out of bed, because the general question elicited lengthy accounts of the birth itself, and prompts about the timing of pain medication were added. Because these changes were minor, the pilot interviews were included in the analysis. Consistent with Interpretive Description, the guides were refined during data collection. After early analysis highlighted night-time episodes, a prompt was added about mobilisation at night and outside physiotherapy hours, and professionals were asked about situations in which their appraisal differed from a woman's. All versions were dated and retained in the audit trail.

Each interview centred on concrete decision episodes. Participants were asked to describe a specific occasion, such as the first time a woman sat up, stood or walked, or a recent occasion when a professional helped a woman to move. For each episode, prompts established the proposed activity, the woman's symptoms and concerns, who contributed to the judgement of readiness, what was communicated and by whom, what reportedly happened, why the activity went ahead or was adapted, paused or deferred, and what later prompted reconsideration. Participants were explicitly invited to describe routine episodes and episodes of agreement as well as difficult encounters. Reconsideration was explored retrospectively within each interview, so repeat interviews were not routinely required, and none were needed.

All interviews were audio-recorded with consent. Immediately afterwards, the interviewer wrote field notes on the setting, non-verbal communication, interruptions and initial analytic impressions. Seven in-hospital interviews were briefly paused for infant feeding or clinical care and resumed with the woman's agreement. Women were offered the choice of being interviewed alone; in five interviews a female relative remained at the woman's request. The presence and any contributions of companions were recorded in field notes and considered during analysis, and only women's own words were quoted.

A brief contextual information form was completed for each participant. For women, it recorded age band, parity, urgency category, type of anaesthesia, whether labour preceded the caesarean, postoperative day at interview, timing of first mobilisation, receipt of physiotherapy and infant location; clinical items were taken from the medical record with consent. For professionals, it recorded role, years of experience, usual shift pattern and approximate frequency of involvement in post-caesarean mobilisation. The complete interview guides and the contextual information form are provided in Supplementary File S1.

## 2.5 Transcription, translation and data management

Recordings were transcribed verbatim in the language of the interview by the interviewing researcher or by a bilingual research assistant bound by a confidentiality agreement. Pauses, hesitations and laughter were noted where they bore on meaning. The interviewer checked every transcript against its recording and corrected errors.

During transcription, names of people, hospitals and wards, dates and other potentially identifying details were removed or replaced. Each participant received a code indicating group and sequence (W01–W18 for women, N01–N12 for nurses and P01–P07 for physiotherapists), and hospital was recorded only in a restricted linkage file. Quotations are not attributed to hospital, because the small number of physiotherapists at each site could otherwise make individuals identifiable. Where a participant described a potentially recognisable incident, non-essential details were altered or omitted without changing meaning.

Twenty-seven interviews were conducted in Arabic (all 18 women, 4 nurses and 5 physiotherapists) and ten in English (8 nurses and 2 physiotherapists). Arabic transcripts were coded in Arabic by the two Arabic-speaking researchers to keep analysis close to participants' original expressions (van Nes et al., 2010). Analytic memos, team discussions and code labels were in English, with original Arabic terms recorded where meaning depended on them. Whole transcripts were not translated. Only quotations selected for publication were translated into English, following recommendations for cross-language qualitative research (Squires, 2009). MAA, who is bilingual and teaches in English-medium nursing education, translated each quotation for meaning rather than word for word. SOA independently compared each translation with the Arabic original, and discrepancies were discussed until agreement was reached. Culturally specific expressions without a direct English equivalent, such as religious expressions of acceptance and colloquial terms for weakness or dizziness, were rendered by meaning, with a transliteration or brief explanation where needed.

Audio files were transferred on the day of each interview from an encrypted recorder to a password-protected, encrypted Jouf University server and then deleted from the recorder. Consent forms were stored in a locked cabinet, and the linkage file in a separate encrypted folder. Only the two researchers had access to identifiable data; the research assistant accessed only the audio files assigned for transcription. Audio recordings will be destroyed after publication. De-identified transcripts, analytic files and the audit trail will be retained for five years after publication in accordance with institutional policy and then securely destroyed.

## 2.6 Data analysis

Analysis followed the inductive, iterative logic of Interpretive Description (Thorne, 2025). Its aim was a coherent interpretive account of how readiness appraisals informed mobilisation decisions and their reconsideration, rather than a list of barriers, facilitators or experiences. Analysis was guided throughout by Thorne's questions: what is happening here, what am I learning about this, and what might this mean for practice.

Analysis began after the first interviews and proceeded alongside data collection, so that early interpretations informed later sampling and guide refinement (Sections 2.3.3 and 2.4). Each transcript was read several times alongside its field notes before coding, so that each account was first understood as a whole. Broad-based inductive coding was then applied to segments describing appraisals, actions, communication, reasons and context. To avoid premature fragmentation, early codes remained close to participants' language and were grouped only once relationships between them became apparent (Thorne, 2025). Analytic memos recorded emerging ideas, questions, links between accounts and the researchers' reactions.

To distinguish readiness appraisals from reported actions and reasons for reconsideration, each concrete decision episode was summarised in an episode matrix. Each row represented one episode and recorded the activity, the recovery stage (first mobilisation or later progression), the appraisals reported and who made them (the woman, a nurse, a physiotherapist or a family member), the information and concerns informing each appraisal, the reported action, the reasons given, and any reconsideration with its prompt. This structure maintained the analytic boundary between what was judged and what was done, and showed where similar appraisals led to different actions or where actions followed one person's appraisal rather than another's. In total, 164 episodes were summarised: 71 from women, 63 from nurses and 30 from physiotherapists.

Constant comparison operated at three levels. Within accounts, analysts examined how reasoning changed across successive episodes and activities, such as a woman judging herself ready to sit but not to stand. Within groups, they examined variation by hospital, experience, parity and clinical course where the data supported such distinctions. Across groups, they compared how women, nurses and physiotherapists explained the weight given to different appraisals and described communication, participation and responsibility. Because accounts were independently recruited (Section 2.3.5), cross-group comparisons were interpreted as patterns of reasoning rather than competing versions of the same encounter. Activity, recovery stage and service context were retained in comparisons where the data supported them; for example, episodes occurring outside physiotherapy hours were compared with daytime episodes.

Codes were grouped into provisional patterns, which were tested against the full data set and the episode matrix and refined through repeated movement between individual episodes and complete accounts. Analysts deliberately sought episodes of agreement, routine episodes and accounts that did not fit emerging patterns, and used them to qualify or revise interpretations. For each major interpretation, the team asked whether alternative explanations, such as staffing pressure rather than clinical judgement, or family preference rather than the woman's own appraisal, could equally account for the data. The distinctions in Figure 1 were themselves examined, including whether appraisals and decisions could be separated in participants' accounts and whether reconsideration followed new information or changes in who was present.

MAA and SOA both coded all transcripts. Coding was not used to calculate agreement. Instead, the analysts compared their coding and memos in analytic meetings to broaden interpretation through their complementary nursing and physiotherapy perspectives, treating differences as prompts to re-examine the data rather than errors to resolve. Data were organised in NVivo 14 (Lumivero), and the episode matrix was maintained in a spreadsheet linked to transcript locations.

## 2.7 Researcher reflexivity and methodological rigour

### 2.7.1 Researcher reflexivity

MAA is a nurse and faculty member in maternal and child health nursing with clinical experience in maternity care and experience of qualitative interviewing with perinatal women. SOA is a physiotherapist and faculty member in physiotherapy with clinical experience in musculoskeletal and women's health rehabilitation. Both have training in qualitative methods, and MAA has previously used Interpretive Description. Both are Saudi women whose first language is Arabic and who are fluent in English.

Neither researcher was employed by or held a clinical, supervisory or managerial role at any participating hospital during the study. Some professional participants may have been former students of Jouf University; the cross-disciplinary allocation of interviews meant that no professional was interviewed by a researcher who had taught them. Participants were told the researchers' names, professions and university affiliation, that the study aimed to understand how decisions about getting up and moving after emergency caesarean birth were made and explained, and that the researchers were not evaluating individual practice or reporting to hospital management. Women were told that the interviewer was a nurse who was not part of their care team.

Before data collection, each researcher wrote a reflexive statement of her assumptions. MAA anticipated that early mobilisation was generally beneficial and that women's reluctance often reflected fear that information could address. SOA anticipated that physiotherapy was under-used and that nursing workload constrained progression. Both expected that women's preferences might receive limited weight. These statements were revisited at each analytic meeting, and each researcher kept a reflexive journal recording reactions to interviews and moments when data challenged expectations (Olmos-Vega et al., 2023). Cross-disciplinary interviewing and joint analysis ensured that each researcher's professional assumptions were questioned by a colleague from the other discipline. These procedures altered the analysis: several women described reluctance as a reasoned judgement about dizziness or bleeding rather than fear, which led the team to abandon "fear" as an umbrella code, and accounts of nurses seeking physiotherapy advice informally qualified the assumption that physiotherapy was under-used.

### 2.7.2 Methodological rigour

Rigour was guided by Thorne's (2025) criteria of epistemological integrity, representative credibility, analytic logic and interpretive authority. Epistemological integrity was supported by alignment between the research question, the constructivist–interpretive position, episode-centred interviewing and analysis, and by restricting claims to reported reasoning. Representative credibility was supported by variation across three hospitals, three participant groups and a range of clinical courses, and by reporting patterns confined to one group or hospital as such. Analytic logic was supported by an audit trail comprising dated interview guide versions, field notes, coded transcripts, the episode matrix, analytic memos, minutes of 11 analytic meetings and successive versions of the interpretive structure. Interpretive authority was supported by recorded consideration of competing explanations, reflexive journals and cross-disciplinary discussion.

The detailed description of the setting, pathways and service differences (Section 2.2), together with aggregated participant characteristics, allows readers to judge transferability to their own services. Quotations were selected from across groups and hospitals to illustrate both typical and contrasting accounts rather than only the most vivid. Preliminary interpretations were reviewed by an experienced qualitative nurse researcher who was not involved in the study. She examined the audit trail and a sample of coded transcripts and challenged the interpretations; her questions led the team to distinguish more sharply between who made a readiness appraisal and whose appraisal determined the action. Preliminary findings were also discussed with the patient and public involvement group (Section 2.9) to check their relevance and clarity for women. This was not member checking: participants were not asked to validate findings, because interpretations synthesised across many accounts would not correspond to any individual's experience (Thorne, 2025).

## 2.8 Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of King Abdulaziz Specialist Hospital, Al-Jouf Health Cluster (committee registration H-13-S-161; approval No. 9804; 15 February 2026), covering Hospitals A and B. Hospital C, outside the cluster, granted separate administrative permission through its research committee before recruitment began there. The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and the regulations of the Saudi National Committee of Bioethics for research involving human subjects.

All participants gave written informed consent covering participation, audio recording and the use of de-identified quotations in publications; women also consented to access to specified items in their medical record. For telephone and video interviews, written consent was obtained beforehand and confirmed verbally at the start of the recording. Women were asked for consent separately from, and at least 12 hours after, the initial information visit.

Participation was voluntary, and participants could decline any question or stop the interview at any time. They could withdraw their data without giving a reason for up to 30 days after interview, after which de-identified data had been integrated into the analysis and removal could no longer be guaranteed; this limit was explained before consent. No participant withdrew. Women were assured that declining or withdrawing would not affect their care, and professionals that their participation and accounts would not be disclosed to managers or used to evaluate their performance. No account was shared with other participants or hospital staff.

Before each in-hospital interview, the woman's nurse confirmed fitness to be interviewed. During interviews, the interviewer watched for pain, fatigue or distress, offered breaks and stopped if a woman asked or appeared unwell. A distress protocol required the interviewer to pause and check on the participant, offer to stop, inform the woman's nurse of any clinical concern with her agreement, and signpost hospital psychological and social support services. Two women became tearful when describing the emergency birth; both chose to continue after a pause, and one accepted information about support services. Professionals describing distressing clinical events were offered pauses and information about staff support services.

In-hospital interviews took place in a private room or, for women unable to leave their bed, behind closed curtains when the bay was quiet. When a woman chose to have a companion present, she was reminded beforehand that the companion would hear her answers and that she could ask to speak privately at any point, and companions were asked to keep the discussion confidential. Professionals' accounts were protected by removing role-specific and site-specific details from quotations, not attributing quotations to hospital, and reporting physiotherapists' characteristics only in aggregate. Because accounts were not linked across participants (Section 2.3.5), no consent for linkage was required, and no participant learned of another's participation.

## 2.9 Patient and public involvement

Patient and public involvement is reported with reference to the GRIPP2 short form (Staniszewska et al., 2017). Four women with previous emergency caesarean birth in Al-Jouf Region, recruited through a university-affiliated community maternal health programme and not study participants, formed an advisory group and were reimbursed for their time and travel. Before ethics submission, the group reviewed the research question, the women's information sheet and consent form, and the draft women's interview guide. As a result, technical terms for mobilisation were replaced with everyday Arabic expressions, a prompt was added about how newborn care and feeding affected decisions to move, and women were offered flexible interview timing and a telephone interview after discharge. During analysis, the group discussed anonymised preliminary interpretations; members recognised the distinction between feeling able to sit and feeling able to stand and highlighted the influence of female relatives in encouraging or discouraging movement, which prompted closer analytic attention to companions' contributions. The group is contributing to a plain-language Arabic summary of the findings for women and maternity services. Public contributors were not involved in conducting interviews or coding data.
