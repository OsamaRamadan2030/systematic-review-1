# When Is Intervention Warranted? Nurse Managers' Judgements About Informal Generative Artificial Intelligence Use in Clinical Nursing in Saudi Arabia — An Interpretive Description

## 4. Methods

### 4.1 Design and Rationale

This study used a qualitative interpretive description design [13]. Interpretive description is a nursing-derived methodology developed to generate practice-relevant knowledge about clinical and organisational phenomena. It allows researchers to draw on disciplinary knowledge while remaining open to what participants describe [13, 17, 19]. The design suited the study for three reasons. First, the research questions concern an applied problem: how nurse managers decide whether, when and how to intervene in informal generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) use. The aim was to produce knowledge that could inform management practice and governance, not to develop formal theory. Second, interpretive description accepts that a clinical phenomenon may look different across settings and people, and it treats this variation as analytically important. That fits the study's interest in how hospital context, managerial level and the clarity of permissions shape judgement. Third, interpretive description allows sensitising theory to be used openly and with reflexive control [17]. This is consistent with how organisational sensemaking and sensegiving [14–16] were used here (Figure 1).

Other qualitative designs were considered and judged less suitable. Grounded theory aims to build a substantive theory, but this study sought an applied interpretive account. Interpretive phenomenology centres on the essence of lived experience, but this study centred on the reasoning within supervisory decisions. Descriptive qualitative designs would have limited the interpretive depth needed to explain why managers act, delay or decide not to act.

The study took a constructivist, naturalistic position. Managers' accounts were treated as situated interpretations of events, not as direct records of what staff did. Data were generated through episode-focused, semi-structured interviews informed by the critical incident technique [20]. Each manager was asked to describe specific, real episodes of informal GenAI use they had encountered, and the reasoning that led to their response. Anchoring interviews in concrete episodes, rather than general opinions about artificial intelligence, was intended to reduce socially desirable answers and to make the logic of decision-making accessible for analysis. The **episode** served as the main unit of comparison in analysis, and the **participant** served as the unit of sampling.

### 4.2 Setting and Context

The study was conducted in four public hospitals in the Jouf region of northern Saudi Arabia. Sites were selected purposively to capture differences in hospital size, level of care, clinical specialities and patient acuity. Bed capacity ranged from 200 to 420, and the hospitals provided secondary or tertiary care, including inpatient and emergency services. All four hospitals held accreditation from the Saudi Central Board for Accreditation of Healthcare Institutions (CBAHI) and operated under a shared regional healthcare directorate. This common governance structure provided a comparable administrative context, while differences in service provision supported examination of contextual variation across sites.

To protect institutional anonymity, the hospitals are referred to as Sites A to D. Each site used an electronic health record and had institutional policies on information security, acceptable use of information technology and clinical documentation. Before data collection, the research team reviewed these policies (with permission from each hospital's nursing directorate and information technology department) to describe the governance context in which managers worked. At the time of the study, none of the four hospitals had approved a GenAI tool for clinical nursing use. None had a policy that named which GenAI tools or tasks were permitted. However, all four sites had existing rules that prohibited entering patient-identifiable information into external, non-approved platforms, in line with national data-protection requirements [9, 10]. This mix of explicit restrictions on some actions and unclear permission for others matched the contrast the study was designed to examine. The policy review was used only to understand the context and to inform interview probes. It was not analysed as primary data.

The study was conducted between April 2026 and September 2026. Interviews took place between April 2026 and August 2026, and analysis continued alongside and after data collection until September 2026.

### 4.3 Participants, Sample and Sampling

#### Eligibility

In this study, a *nurse manager* was defined as a registered nurse who holds formal line-management responsibility for registered nursing staff working in direct clinical care. This included first-line managers (head nurses and unit managers) and middle managers (nursing supervisors and assistant directors of nursing with responsibility for clinical areas).

Inclusion criteria were:

1. a current nurse manager role, as defined above, in one of the four participating hospitals;
2. at least 12 months' experience in a nursing management role, so that participants could draw on a range of supervisory situations;
3. direct supervisory responsibility for registered nurses in inpatient, critical care, emergency or outpatient clinical areas;
4. experience of at least one episode, within the previous 12 months, in which they became aware of, were told about or had reason to suspect informal GenAI use by nursing staff for a work-related purpose; and
5. ability to take part in an interview in Arabic or English.

Exclusion criteria were: (a) a role with no line-management responsibility for clinical nursing staff (for example, roles limited to education, quality or infection control); (b) current membership of the research team or a current academic or employment relationship with any researcher (for example, enrolment in a course taught by a team member); and (c) extended leave during the data-collection period.

Criterion 4 was required because the study focused on episodes. Managers who had never encountered or suspected informal use could not provide the episode-level data that the research questions require. The 12-month window was chosen to support detailed recall while capturing the recent spread of GenAI tools.

#### Sampling strategy

Purposive, maximum-variation sampling [21] was used to include managers whose judgements were likely to differ in meaningful ways. A sampling matrix was set up before recruitment and monitored throughout. It covered:

- **hospital site** (Sites A–D; at least five participants per site);
- **managerial level** (first-line and middle management);
- **clinical area** (medical, surgical, critical care, emergency, maternity and paediatrics, outpatient and other specialities);
- **years of management experience** (fewer than 5 years, 5–10 years, more than 10 years);
- **gender** and **nationality** (Saudi and non-Saudi nurse managers), reflecting the composition of the regional nursing workforce; and
- **type of episode encountered** (use involving an explicit restriction, use where permission was unclear, or both).

As analysis progressed, sampling also became partly theoretical in the sense used in interpretive description [13]. Later recruitment gave priority to managers who were likely to add contrasting or under-represented episodes. Examples included managers in high-acuity areas, managers who had escalated an episode, and managers who had chosen not to intervene.

#### Sample size

Sample size was planned using the principle of information power [22] and checked against empirical evidence on sample adequacy in interview studies [23–25]. Information power proposes that the more relevant information a sample holds, the fewer participants are needed. It depends on five dimensions: study aim, sample specificity, use of established theory, quality of dialogue and analysis strategy. Table 1 summarises how each dimension was assessed for this study.

**Table 1. Assessment of information power used to plan the sample size**

| Dimension [22] | Study characteristic | Effect on required sample |
|---|---|---|
| Aim | Moderately broad: three research questions covering recognition, judgement, communication and support needs | Increases the number needed |
| Sample specificity | Dense: all participants held managerial responsibility and had direct experience of relevant episodes | Decreases the number needed |
| Established theory | Sensitising concepts (organisational sensemaking and sensegiving) used to guide enquiry, not to test hypotheses | Moderate effect |
| Quality of dialogue | Episode-anchored interviews by experienced interviewers, in the participant's preferred language | Decreases the number needed |
| Analysis strategy | Cross-case, cross-episode comparison across four sites and two types of permission clarity | Increases the number needed |

Taken together, these dimensions indicated a moderate sample. Empirical evidence offers a further reference point. A systematic review of empirical tests found that saturation in interview studies with relatively homogeneous samples is usually reached within 9 to 17 interviews [23]. Code saturation has been reported at about 9 interviews, while meaning saturation, which is the full understanding of issues, needed 16 to 24 interviews [24]. Because this study needed comparison across four sites, two management levels and two categories of permission clarity, a target range of **20 to 28 participants** was set in advance. This range allowed at least five participants per site and gave enough episodes in both the explicit-restriction and unclear-permission categories to support comparison.

The decision to stop recruitment did not rely on numbers alone. It was based on the analytic sufficiency of the data for answering the research questions, which fits the interpretive aims of the study [13, 26]. Recruitment ended when all three of the following conditions were met:

1. the sampling matrix was filled, with at least five participants from each site and representation across management levels and clinical areas;
2. each of the two permission categories (explicit restriction and unclear permission) included at least ten detailed episodes with enough information about context, reasoning and response for comparison; and
3. in the three most recent interviews, no new type of episode or dimension of reasoning appeared that changed the developing interpretive account. This was assessed with a stopping-criterion approach adapted from Guest and colleagues [25] and recorded in analytic memos.

These conditions were met after 25 interviews. The final sample therefore included **25 nurse managers**, which falls within the planned range and is consistent with the evidence above.

### 4.4 Recruitment and Consent

After ethical and institutional approval was obtained, the nursing directorate at each hospital gave the study coordinator a list of nurse managers who met the role criteria (criteria 1 to 3). Hospital administrators played no further role in recruitment. They were not told who was invited, who agreed or who declined. This step helped prevent pressure to take part and protected participants from being identified within their organisation.

The study coordinator, who had no employment or line-management relationship with any eligible manager, sent invitations by institutional email. Each invitation included a bilingual (Arabic and English) participant information sheet. The information sheet explained the study aim, what participation involved, how confidentiality would be protected, how data would be handled, the limits of confidentiality (see Section 4.10) and the right to withdraw. Interested managers contacted the coordinator directly. The coordinator then confirmed eligibility, including criterion 4, in a short screening conversation. To build the planned variation in the sample, invitations were sent in waves and guided by the sampling matrix.

Of 38 managers identified as meeting the role criteria, 31 were invited across the recruitment waves. Of these, 25 agreed to take part, 4 declined because of workload and 2 did not respond after one reminder. The other 7 eligible managers were not invited because their profile was already well represented in the sampling matrix when recruitment ended. No participant withdrew after the interview.

Participants had at least 72 hours to read the information sheet and ask questions before giving consent. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection. For interviews conducted by videoconference, the signed consent form was returned electronically before the interview. At the start of each interview, the interviewer confirmed consent verbally, including consent to audio recording. No financial or other incentive was offered.

### 4.5 Data Collection

#### Interview guide

Data were collected through individual, semi-structured, episode-focused interviews. The interview guide (Supplementary File 1) was developed from the research questions, the literature reviewed in the Introduction, the national governance context [7–10] and the critical incident technique [20]. It had five parts:

1. **Opening and role context:** the participant's role, unit and how GenAI is talked about in their workplace.
2. **Episode elicitation:** a request to describe, in their own words, one to three specific episodes of informal GenAI use they had encountered. For each episode, this covered how it came to their attention, the nursing task involved, the information entered and what they understood about local permissions.
3. **Judgement and response:** what the manager considered, what they did or chose not to do, when they did it, who they consulted and why.
4. **Communication and reconsideration:** how they explained their response to staff and others, how others reacted and whether they later changed their view or response.
5. **Support and governance:** the knowledge, authority, expertise and institutional arrangements they considered necessary for effective oversight.

Opening questions in each part were open and non-leading. Probes informed by sensemaking and sensegiving (for example, about cues that something needed attention, or about efforts to shape how staff understood an episode) were used only after the participant had described an episode in their own terms. This sequence was chosen so that theory would not shape participants' first accounts [17]. The guide was prepared in English, translated into Arabic by a bilingual team member and checked by a second bilingual team member. Differences were resolved through discussion to ensure conceptual equivalence, not word-for-word correspondence [27].

#### Pilot interviews

The guide was piloted with two nurse managers from a public hospital outside the study sites who met the role criteria. One pilot was in Arabic and one in English. Following the pilot, the order of parts 2 and 3 was changed so that judgement questions followed directly from each episode. A prompt was also added to ask about episodes in which the manager decided not to intervene. Pilot data were not included in the analysis.

#### Interview procedures

Before the interview, participants were asked to think of specific episodes to discuss. They were reminded in writing not to share the names of staff or patients, or any other identifying details. A short demographic and professional questionnaire recorded age group, gender, nationality, managerial level, clinical area, years of nursing and management experience, highest qualification and any training received on artificial intelligence.

Interviews were conducted by two members of the research team (one female and one male) with doctoral-level training in qualitative methods. Participants could choose the interviewer's gender, the interview language and the interview format. Twenty interviews took place face to face in a private room away from the participant's clinical unit and outside handover times. Five took place by secure, institution-licensed videoconference (Microsoft Teams), with the participant in a private space [28]. Seventeen interviews were conducted in Arabic and eight in English. Interviews lasted between 46 and 84 minutes (median 62 minutes) and were audio-recorded with consent.

Interviewers wrote field notes straight after each interview. These recorded non-verbal features, the interview atmosphere, initial analytic impressions and reflexive observations. Data collection and analysis ran alongside each other, so the guide was refined between interviews to follow up on new lines of enquiry. For example, from the ninth interview onward, a probe was added about the difference between "checking the output" and "checking what was entered". All changes to the guide were dated and recorded in the audit trail.

At the end of each interview, participants were asked whether they would agree to review a short summary of preliminary interpretations later in the study (see Section 4.8).

### 4.6 Data Management

Data were managed in line with the Personal Data Protection Law and its Implementing Regulation [9, 10] and Jouf University data-governance policy.

- **Recording and storage:** Interviews were recorded on encrypted digital recorders. Within 24 hours, files were transferred to password-protected, encrypted storage on the Jouf University secure server and then deleted from the recorder. Signed consent forms and the linkage key were stored separately from interview data. Only the principal investigator could access the linkage key.
- **Pseudonymisation:** Each participant received an identification code made up of a site code and participant number (for example, B-NM07). During transcription, all names, unit identifiers, staff and patient details and any other potentially identifying information were removed or replaced with generic descriptors (for example, "[staff nurse]" or "[surgical unit]").
- **Transcription:** Recordings were transcribed verbatim in the language of the interview by two trained research assistants who had signed confidentiality agreements. The interviewer checked each transcript against the audio recording for accuracy and completeness.
- **Translation:** Arabic transcripts were analysed in Arabic so that meaning was kept close to the participants' own words, and codes were written in English. Excerpts chosen for reporting were translated into English by a bilingual team member and checked against the Arabic original by a second bilingual team member. The aim was to preserve meaning, idiom and tone, not literal wording [27]. Disagreements were resolved by discussion and recorded.
- **Exclusion of generative AI from data handling:** Given the study topic and the confidentiality of the data, no GenAI or cloud-based automated tool was used for transcription, translation, coding or interpretation. Only the research team had access to the data.
- **Software:** NVivo 14 (Lumivero) was used to organise data, codes, memos and analytic matrices. The project file was stored on the secure university server.
- **Retention:** Data will be kept for five years after publication, in line with institutional policy, and then securely destroyed. Audio recordings were destroyed once transcript accuracy had been confirmed.

### 4.7 Data Analysis

Analysis followed the interpretive description approach [13, 19]. It began with the first interview and was iterative throughout. Analysis moved from close reading of individual accounts, to comparison across episodes and participants, to a coherent interpretive account that answered the research questions. Analysis involved five overlapping phases (Table 2).

**Table 2. Analytic phases**

| Phase | Activities | Outputs |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Immersion | Repeated listening to recordings and reading of transcripts and field notes; initial reflexive memos on first impressions and possible influence of the researchers' assumptions | Interview summaries; reflexive memos |
| 2. Broad-based inductive coding | Line-by-line coding of transcripts close to participants' language, without imposing sensitising concepts; two researchers independently coded the first five transcripts and met to compare and discuss interpretations | Shared, evolving coding framework with definitions |
| 3. Episode-level analysis | Each episode was extracted and described in an episode matrix covering: how it came to the manager's attention, the nursing task, information entered, permission status (explicit restriction or unclear permission), perceived risk, safeguards available, response (intervention, non-intervention, seeking further information, escalation), justification, communication and later reconsideration | Episode matrix in NVivo [29] |
| 4. Constant comparison | Comparison within and across participants, episodes, sites, managerial levels and permission categories; focused search for divergent and negative cases; examining when and how sensitising concepts helped explain the data, and where they did not | Comparative memos; refined patterns |
| 5. Interpretive synthesis | Developing an integrated account of how managers judge whether, when and how to intervene; testing the account against the full data set; linking it to implications for practice and governance | Final interpretive account; selection of illustrative excerpts |

Several features of the analysis deserve emphasis.

- **Use of sensitising concepts:** Sensemaking and sensegiving [14–16] were used as sensitising concepts [30], not as a coding framework. Codes were developed inductively first (phase 2). The concepts were brought in only during comparison (phase 4), to raise questions about the data. Reflexive memos documented each time a concept was used, whether it explained the data, needed modification or was set aside [18]. Accounts that did not fit, such as responses that did not involve any deliberate attempt to shape others' understanding, were kept and analysed in their own right.
- **Comparison by permission clarity:** The episode matrix made it possible to compare episodes involving explicit restrictions with those involving unclear permissions systematically. This comparison directly addressed research question 2 and the third objective.
- **Divergent cases:** Episodes and participants that contradicted emerging patterns (for example, managers who tolerated use that others would have stopped) were actively sought and used to refine the interpretation, not set aside.
- **Team analysis:** The full team met every two weeks during analysis to discuss codes, matrices and developing interpretations. Consistent with the interpretive paradigm, the aim of having two coders was to deepen interpretation through discussion, not to reach statistical agreement. For this reason, inter-rater reliability coefficients were not calculated [26, 31]. Disagreements were resolved by returning to the data and were recorded in memos.

### 4.8 Rigour and Trustworthiness

Trustworthiness was addressed using Lincoln and Guba's criteria [32], alongside Thorne's quality criteria for interpretive description: epistemological integrity, representative credibility, analytic logic and interpretive authority [13]. The strategies used are described below and summarised in Table 3.

#### Credibility

Credibility was supported by prolonged engagement with the data across the six-month study period and by interview procedures that encouraged detailed, honest accounts. These included anchoring interviews in specific episodes, offering a choice of language and interviewer gender, and giving clear assurances about confidentiality. Investigator triangulation was achieved through independent coding of initial transcripts and regular team discussion. Data were triangulated across four sites, two management levels and a range of clinical areas. The policy review (Section 4.2) gave contextual triangulation for how participants described the permission environment.

Negative case analysis was carried out throughout. Peer debriefing took place with an experienced qualitative nurse researcher outside the research team, who reviewed the coding framework, episode matrix and draft interpretation at two points.

Member reflections [33, 34] were used in place of transcript return. A two-page summary of preliminary interpretations, in Arabic and English, was sent to the 21 participants who had agreed to review it, and 14 responded. Their comments were treated as further data that could deepen or challenge the interpretation, not as a test of whether it was "correct". For example, several participants pointed out the role of informal consultation with peers before formal escalation, and this was developed further in the final account.

#### Dependability

A detailed audit trail was kept. It included the dated versions of the interview guide, the coding framework and its revisions, the episode matrix, analytic and reflexive memos, minutes of team meetings and records of analytic decisions. Data collection and analysis procedures were standardised through an interviewer briefing and joint review of the first two interviews by both interviewers. The audit trail was reviewed by a team member who had not conducted interviews, to check that analytic decisions were traceable and consistent.

#### Confirmability

Confirmability was supported by grounding interpretations in the data and by reflexivity (Section 4.9). Each interpretive claim was linked to supporting excerpts from several participants. Excerpts are reported with participant codes so that readers can see the range of sources behind each claim. Reflexive journals and memos recorded how the researchers' backgrounds and the sensitising concepts may have influenced interpretation, and how these influences were examined.

#### Transferability

Transferability was supported by thick description [32] of the national governance context, the hospitals, the policy environment and the characteristics of participants and episodes. This allows readers to judge how relevant the findings are to their own settings. The maximum-variation sample and the analysis of divergent cases were intended to show the range of managerial reasoning, not a single typical pattern. The final account will be presented together with the conditions under which its patterns appeared (for example, managerial level or permission clarity), so that readers can consider how far it applies to other healthcare systems with similar digital transformation and data-protection requirements.

**Table 3. Summary of trustworthiness strategies**

| Criterion | Strategies |
|---|---|
| Credibility | Prolonged engagement; episode-anchored interviewing; data and investigator triangulation; contextual policy review; negative case analysis; external peer debriefing; member reflections |
| Dependability | Audit trail; dated versions of guide and coding framework; standardised procedures; audit by a non-interviewing team member |
| Confirmability | Reflexive journals and memos; interpretations linked to multiple data sources; transparent reporting of excerpts |
| Transferability | Thick description of context, sites, participants and episodes; maximum-variation sampling; reporting the conditions under which patterns appeared |
| Interpretive description criteria [13] | Epistemological integrity (alignment of question, design and analysis); representative credibility (variation across sites and roles); analytic logic (audit trail); interpretive authority (reflexivity and member reflections) |

### 4.9 Reflexivity and Positionality

The research team was made up of four nurse researchers based at Jouf University. All held doctoral degrees and had experience in qualitative research. Two had worked previously as nurse managers in Saudi public hospitals, one had expertise in nursing informatics and digital health, and one in nursing ethics and governance. Two team members were Saudi and two were non-Saudi Arabic speakers, and the team included both women and men. No team member worked at any participating hospital or had an employment or supervisory relationship with any participant.

The team recognised that its position could shape the research in several ways [35]. Team members with nurse management experience brought insider understanding of supervisory pressures, which helped build rapport and design relevant probes. However, this experience could also lead them to assume shared meanings or to identify with managers' justifications. The team member with informatics expertise brought knowledge of GenAI capabilities and risks, which could have led to judging participants' technical understanding. All team members had used GenAI tools in academic work. They held neither strongly supportive nor strongly opposing views about its use in clinical nursing, but they acknowledged that their views on appropriate use could influence how they interpreted managers' decisions.

As university academics, the researchers may have been seen by participants as linked to formal authority or to an evaluation of their decisions. This could encourage participants to present their responses in a favourable light. To reduce this risk, interviewers explained at the start of every interview that the study did not judge whether managers' decisions were right or wrong. They also stressed that no information would be shared with the hospitals, and they avoided showing approval or disapproval of reported actions.

Reflexivity was practised throughout the study. Each team member kept a reflexive journal. Interviewers wrote reflexive field notes after each interview. During team meetings, the team discussed how members' backgrounds might be influencing interpretation. In particular, members with management experience were invited to question interpretations that closely matched their own past practice, and members without management experience were invited to question interpretations that seemed to judge managers too quickly. These discussions were recorded in the audit trail.

### 4.10 Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Jouf University Institutional Review Board (Protocol No. 7603) and from the ethics committees of all participating hospitals prior to recruitment. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection. Participation was voluntary, and participants were informed of their right to withdraw at any stage without occupational consequences.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki [36] and the Saudi Law of Ethics of Research on Living Creatures and its Implementing Regulations [37]. Participants could withdraw their data up to the point at which data analysis was completed (30 September 2026), without giving a reason. The study raised four particular ethical issues, which were addressed as follows.

**Protection from occupational risk.** Participants described their own supervisory decisions, some of which could be seen as departing from policy. To protect them, hospital administrators were not told who took part, interviews took place away from clinical units, and all data were pseudonymised. Findings are reported so that no individual, unit or site can be identified. Where a combination of characteristics could identify a participant (for example, a rare clinical area at a particular site), these details are reported in general terms.

**Third parties.** Participants described the conduct of staff who had not consented to the study. To protect these staff, participants were asked in advance and during the interview not to name or identify staff or patients. Any identifying details mentioned by mistake were removed during transcription. Episodes are reported only at a level of detail that does not allow individuals to be identified.

**Limits of confidentiality and disclosure.** The information sheet and consent form explained that confidentiality would be kept unless a participant disclosed an ongoing situation posing a serious risk of harm to patients that had not already been addressed through institutional procedures. A disclosure protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board. If such a disclosure occurred, the interviewer would pause the interview, discuss the concern with the participant and encourage them to report it through the appropriate institutional channels. The researchers would not report it themselves unless required by law. No disclosure requiring this protocol occurred during the study.

**Participant well-being and data protection.** Discussing difficult supervisory decisions might cause discomfort. Interviewers were prepared to pause or stop interviews, and participants could skip any question. Interviews were scheduled at times convenient to participants and did not interfere with clinical duties. Data were managed in line with the Personal Data Protection Law [9, 10], as described in Section 4.6.

The study is reported following the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) [38] and the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) [39]. Completed checklists are provided in Supplementary File 2.
