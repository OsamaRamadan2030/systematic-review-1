# 4. Methods

## 4.1 Design and Rationale

The study used a qualitative interpretive description design to develop a practice-relevant understanding of unit-level nurse managers' reported judgements and responses to nurses' care concerns in Saudi hospitals (Thorne, 2025). Interpretive description was chosen because the study addressed an applied question arising from nursing management practice; its purpose was to produce knowledge that managers, quality leaders and educators could use, rather than to generate formal theory (as in grounded theory) or describe the essence of a lived experience (as in phenomenology) (Thorne et al., 1997; Thorne, 2025). The design rests on a constructivist, naturalistic orientation. Managers' accounts were treated as situated professional reasoning, not as objective records of what happened in each case.

Participants were nurses with formal managerial responsibility for a clinical unit. The approach supported interpretation of patterns and variation in professional reasoning within organisational contexts. The conceptual orientation outlined in the Introduction (soft intelligence, problem-sensing versus comfort-seeking, and burden of proof) acted as sensitising concepts (Blumer, 1954). They shaped interview probes about evidence, responsibility and follow-up, but they were not used as a predetermined coding framework. Analysis proceeded inductively through comparisons within and across managers' accounts, so interpretations could challenge or extend the initial sensitising concepts. The inquiry remained open to concerns that managers accepted without further substantiation as well as those that prompted further investigation.

## 4.2 Setting and Context

The study was conducted in six public hospitals in the Jouf region of northern Saudi Arabia. Sites were selected purposively to capture differences in hospital size, level of care, clinical specialities and patient acuity. Bed capacity ranged from 200 to 420, and the hospitals provided secondary or tertiary care, including inpatient and emergency services. All six hospitals held accreditation from the Saudi Central Board for Accreditation of Healthcare Institutions (CBAHI) and operated under a shared regional healthcare directorate. This common governance structure provided a comparable administrative context, while differences in service provision supported examination of contextual variation across sites.

In all six hospitals, clinical units reported a common set of quality and patient-safety indicators to the hospital quality department each month. These included nursing-sensitive indicators (inpatient falls, falls with injury, hospital-acquired pressure injuries, central line-associated bloodstream infection, catheter-associated urinary tract infection and medication-administration incidents), incident-reporting counts and patient-experience scores. Results were displayed on unit and hospital dashboards, compared with organisational targets and national benchmarks, and discussed at monthly unit and hospital quality meetings, consistent with national guidance on indicator validation and benchmarking (Saudi Patient Safety Center, 2025). Unit-level nurse managers (locally titled *head nurse* or *nurse manager*) were responsible for unit indicator performance and reported to the hospital Director of Nursing. Nursing workforces in the study hospitals included both Saudi and internationally educated nurses, and English and Arabic were both used for professional communication.

## 4.3 Participants, Sample and Sampling

**Eligibility.** Participants were registered nurses who:

- held a formal, substantive managerial post (head nurse or nurse manager) with 24-hour accountability for a clinical inpatient, critical care or emergency unit;
- had at least six months' experience in their current managerial role, so they could describe at least one full cycle of indicator reporting and review;
- managed a unit that reported routine quality indicators to the hospital quality department; and
- could take part in an interview in Arabic or English.

Acting or temporary managers in post for less than six months, nurse managers of non-clinical or outpatient-only areas, and any manager with a current supervisory, employment or academic relationship with a member of the research team were excluded.

**Sampling strategy.** Purposive maximum-variation sampling was used to capture a broad range of managerial reasoning across organisational contexts (Thorne, 2025). A sampling matrix was set up before recruitment and monitored during data collection. It covered four dimensions: (a) hospital (all six sites); (b) unit type (medical, surgical, critical care, emergency, paediatric and maternity); (c) years of managerial experience (<5, 5–10 and >10 years); and (d) country of nursing education (Saudi Arabia or abroad). As analysis progressed, sampling also became theoretically informed. For example, managers of units with recently "green" dashboards and managers who had recently escalated a concern were sought to deepen emerging comparisons.

**Sample size.** The sample size was planned on the basis of information power (Malterud et al., 2016) and was not fixed in advance. Information power was judged to be relatively high for four reasons: (a) the aim was narrow and focused on a specific decision problem; (b) participants had high specificity, because all held the role and experience under study; (c) sensitising concepts from established literature were available; and (d) interviews anchored on concrete recent incidents were expected to produce rich, focused dialogue. The cross-case analytic strategy, which compared reasoning across six hospitals and six unit types, required a larger sample than single-case analysis. Empirical studies of saturation in interview research report code saturation at about 9–17 interviews in relatively homogeneous samples, with meaning saturation, meaning a full understanding of the issues raised, requiring about 16–24 interviews (Hennink et al., 2017; Hennink & Kaiser, 2022). On this basis, a provisional range of 18 to 25 participants was set, with at least three participants per hospital and at least two per unit type.

The final sample size was determined by analytic sufficiency, not by a numerical rule (Braun & Clarke, 2021; Thorne, 2025). Two criteria were set in advance and both had to be met: (a) every sampling-matrix cell for hospital and unit type was represented; and (b) the analysts agreed that the three most recent interviews had added no new conceptual dimensions to the developing interpretations relevant to the four objectives, only further illustration of dimensions already identified. Both criteria were met at interview 23, and recruitment ended there. The final sample was **23 unit-level nurse managers** across the six hospitals (three to five per hospital). It included medical (*n* = 5), surgical (*n* = 5), critical care (*n* = 5), emergency (*n* = 3), maternity (*n* = 3) and paediatric (*n* = 2) units. Participant characteristics are reported in Results (Table 1).

## 4.4 Recruitment and Consent

After ethical approval, written permission was obtained from the regional healthcare directorate and from the medical and nursing directors of each hospital (January 2026). At each site, the Director of Nursing acted as gatekeeper only. They provided an anonymised list of the number and unit types of eligible managers (*N* = 38 across the six hospitals) and forwarded the study invitation, participant information sheet and researcher contact details to eligible managers by official hospital email. Managers who were interested contacted the research team directly, so gatekeepers did not know who took part. Invitations were sent in waves aligned with the sampling matrix. Of the 31 managers invited, 26 expressed interest. Three of these were not interviewed: two could not be scheduled before data collection closed because of annual leave, and one withdrew before the interview citing workload. No participant withdrew after the interview.

The participant information sheet, available in Arabic and English, explained the study purpose, voluntary participation, the single interview, audio recording, data handling, the optional member reflection and the right to withdraw. It stated that participation or non-participation would not be disclosed to the employer and would have no effect on employment or appraisal. Prospective participants had at least 48 hours to consider participation and ask questions. Written informed consent was obtained before each interview, and consent to audio recording was confirmed verbally at the start of the recording. Participants could withdraw their data without giving a reason until 14 days after their interview, after which de-identified data were integrated into the analysis. No incentives were offered.

## 4.5 Data Collection

Data were collected between January and June 2026 through one semi-structured, in-depth interview per participant, together with a brief demographic and professional-profile questionnaire. The questionnaire covered age band, gender, nationality group, country of nursing education, highest nursing qualification, total nursing experience, managerial experience, unit type, unit bed capacity and hospital level of care.

**Interview guide.** The interview guide (Supplementary File 1) was developed from the study objectives, the literature reviewed in the Introduction and the sensitising concepts. Its sections were as follows:

1. Unit context and the quality indicators the manager used and reported.
2. A critical-incident prompt (Flanagan, 1954) asking the manager to describe a recent occasion, within the past 12 months, when a nurse raised a care concern while unit indicators appeared reassuring.
3. Probes on how the concern was received and interpreted; what made it credible or not; whether further information was sought, what kind and from whom; who was expected to obtain it; and what investigation, action, escalation, follow-up and feedback followed.
4. A contrasting incident, such as a concern acted on without further substantiation or one not pursued.
5. Organisational conditions that enabled or constrained the manager's judgement and response, including staffing, authority, time, quality-meeting processes and senior-leadership expectations.

Anchoring questions in specific incidents was intended to reduce generalised or socially desirable accounts of "how things should be done".

Two senior nurse leaders and one qualitative methodologist reviewed the guide for relevance, clarity and cultural appropriateness. It was prepared in English and translated into Arabic by a bilingual nurse researcher. A second bilingual researcher back-translated it, and discrepancies were resolved by discussion to keep conceptual rather than literal equivalence (Squires, 2009). The guide was piloted with two nurse managers from a Jouf hospital that was not a study site. Pilot interviews led to minor changes in wording and question order, and pilot data were not included in the analysis. During data collection, probes were refined iteratively within the scope of the guide in response to developing analysis, for example by adding probes on informal channels such as messaging applications through which concerns were raised. All changes were dated and recorded in the audit trail.

**Interview procedures.** All interviews were conducted by the first author, who was trained and experienced in qualitative interviewing and had no supervisory, employment or evaluative relationship with any participant. Each participant chose the interview language (Arabic, *n* = 14; English, *n* = 9) and format. Eighteen interviews took place face to face in a private room within the participant's hospital, away from their unit, and five took place by secure, institutionally licensed video conference (Microsoft Teams) with only the participant and interviewer present. Interviews were scheduled outside peak clinical activity. They lasted 42 to 81 minutes (mean 58 minutes) and were audio-recorded on two encrypted digital recorders. Participants were asked not to name patients or colleagues; any identifiers mentioned were removed during transcription. Immediately after each interview, the interviewer wrote field notes on setting, non-verbal cues, rapport, emerging ideas and reflexive observations. These notes informed both later interviews and analysis.

## 4.6 Data Management

Data were handled in accordance with the Saudi Personal Data Protection Law (Kingdom of Saudi Arabia, 2021) and institutional data-governance policies. All data were stored within Saudi Arabia, and no third-party cloud, automated transcription or generative-AI services were used at any stage.

- **Storage.** Audio files were transferred on the day of the interview to an encrypted, password-protected, institutionally managed drive accessible only to the research team and were then deleted from the recorders. Consent forms and the participant identification key were kept separately: paper forms in a locked cabinet, and the electronic key in a separate encrypted file accessible only to the principal investigator.
- **Transcription.** Interviews were transcribed verbatim in the language of the interview by a professional bilingual transcriptionist based in Saudi Arabia (domestic transcription), who signed a confidentiality agreement and worked on an encrypted institutional device. The interviewer checked each transcript against its audio recording for accuracy.
- **De-identification.** During verification, transcripts were de-identified. Names, unit names, hospital names, dates and potentially identifying events were replaced with codes (participants P01–P23; hospitals H1–H6) or generic descriptors.
- **Research support roles.** Two support roles, beyond the research team, had confidential access to de-identified transcripts only, under signed confidentiality agreements: (a) a bilingual nurse researcher who checked the translation of Arabic quotations into English; and (b) an independent qualitative methodologist who acted as peer debriefer and audited the analytic trail. Neither had any relationship with the study sites.
- **Retention.** Audio recordings were deleted once transcripts had been verified and de-identified. De-identified transcripts, analytic files and the audit trail will be kept for five years after publication in line with institutional policy and then securely destroyed. Because of the small, identifiable population of managers in one region, transcripts will not be shared publicly.

## 4.7 Data Analysis

Analysis followed the iterative, inductive logic of interpretive description (Thorne, 2025) and ran alongside data collection (February to August 2026), so that early interpretations could inform later sampling and probing. NVivo 14 (Lumivero) was used to organise data, codes, memos and matrices. Arabic transcripts were analysed in Arabic by bilingual researchers using English code labels, which avoided loss of meaning from translating whole transcripts. Only the quotations selected for reporting were translated into English. The first author translated them and the bilingual support researcher checked them for conceptual equivalence (Squires, 2009).

Analysis proceeded in six overlapping steps:

1. **Immersion.** The first and second authors listened to the recordings and read each transcript and its field notes several times. They wrote initial analytic memos asking what was happening in each account and why.
2. **Broad initial coding.** The first four transcripts were coded independently by the two analysts using open, descriptive and interpretive codes close to the data. Codes were compared and discussed to produce a provisional coding framework. Because the aim was interpretive depth rather than consensus on fixed categories, inter-coder reliability statistics were not calculated. Differences in interpretation were treated as analytic material and settled through discussion.
3. **Constant comparison.** Each new transcript was coded and compared within and across accounts. Matrices compared reasoning by hospital, unit type, managerial experience, type of concern (for example staffing, missed care, patient deterioration, equipment or professional behaviour) and outcome (concern accepted, investigated, escalated or not pursued). The provisional framework was revised as understanding developed, and all versions were kept.
4. **Engagement with sensitising concepts.** After inductive coding of the first eight interviews, the developing codes were deliberately compared with the sensitising concepts. This comparison showed where managers' accounts supported, complicated or contradicted the ideas of burden of proof, soft intelligence and problem-sensing versus comfort-seeking. Data that did not fit these concepts were actively retained and explored.
5. **Negative and contrasting case analysis.** Accounts in which concerns were accepted without further substantiation, or in which indicators were later found to have missed a problem, were examined systematically to test and refine developing interpretations.
6. **Interpretive synthesis.** Codes were grouped into broader patterns and then into an interpretive account of how managers judged and responded to concerns. The analysts repeatedly asked, "What does this mean for nursing management practice?" (Thorne, 2025). Developing interpretations were discussed at fortnightly analytic meetings of the full research team, ten in total, and with the peer debriefer.

The account was structured around the four objectives but was not limited by them. The final output was a set of interpretive themes and a practice-oriented framework for assessing concerns alongside indicators.

## 4.8 Rigour and Trustworthiness

Rigour was addressed through Thorne's (2025) criteria for interpretive description (epistemological integrity, representative credibility, analytic logic and interpretive authority) and through Lincoln and Guba's (1985) trustworthiness criteria, as described below.

**Credibility.** Interviews grounded in incidents gave concrete, detailed accounts. Maximum-variation sampling across six hospitals and six unit types allowed patterns to be compared across contexts. Credibility was strengthened through independent coding of early transcripts by two analysts, regular team analytic meetings, peer debriefing with an independent qualitative methodologist, and systematic negative-case analysis. All 23 participants were offered optional member reflection (Smith & McGannon, 2018): a two-page plain-language summary of preliminary interpretations in Arabic and English, with an invitation to comment by email or a brief call within three weeks. Ten participants responded. Their reflections were treated as further data to refine and extend interpretations, not as validation of a "correct" account. They led to clarification of one theme on informal follow-up practices.

**Dependability.** An audit trail recorded sampling decisions, versions of the interview guide, dated versions of the coding framework, analytic memos, meeting notes and the rationale for analytic decisions. The independent methodologist reviewed the audit trail at two points: after interview 12 and at the end of analysis.

**Confirmability.** Each interpretation was linked to supporting data through coded excerpts and participant-coded quotations (for example P07, H3) drawn from across the sample. A reflexive journal (Section 4.9) and discussion between analysts with different professional backgrounds were used to identify and challenge assumptions.

**Transferability.** Thick description is provided of the governance and indicator-reporting context (Section 4.2), the sampling matrix and participant characteristics (Table 1), and the conditions under which concerns were raised and judged. This allows readers to judge whether the findings apply to other hospitals and health systems that use performance indicators.

## 4.9 Reflexivity and Positionality

The research team consisted of the first author (R1), a nurse academic with doctoral training in qualitative methods who had previously held a unit-level nurse manager post in a Saudi hospital outside the study region; the second author (R2), a nursing researcher experienced in qualitative health services research with no clinical role in Saudi Arabia; and the third author (R3), a nurse leader with experience in hospital quality and patient safety. Team members had no employment, supervisory or evaluative relationships with participants or study sites.

R1's insider position as a former nurse manager helped build rapport and understand the vocabulary of indicator reporting. It also risked assuming shared meanings and showing sympathy for managerial constraints. To reduce this risk, R1 used clarifying probes (for example "What do you mean by 'proof' here?"), avoided judging participants' decisions, and kept a reflexive journal after each interview and analytic session (Olmos-Vega et al., 2023). R2's outsider perspective and R3's quality-governance perspective were used deliberately in analytic meetings to question interpretations, particularly any that appeared to excuse or criticise managers.

The team recognised that managers might present their responses favourably because of hierarchical and accountability pressures. This was addressed by stressing confidentiality, explaining that the study was not an evaluation of individual performance, asking for both "successful" and "difficult" incidents, and paying attention to hesitations and contradictions in accounts. Reflexive notes were reviewed during analysis and are summarised in the audit trail.

## 4.10 Ethical Considerations

The Institutional Review Board of King Abdulaziz Specialist Hospital, Aljouf Health Cluster (committee registration H-13-S-161), approved the study (No. 9804, 26 December 2025). The approval covered one interview per participant, domestic transcription, optional member reflection, and confidential access to de-identified transcripts by the research support roles specified above. Administrative permission was also obtained from the regional healthcare directorate and each participating hospital.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (World Medical Association, 2025), the Saudi Law of Ethics of Research on Living Creatures and its implementing regulations (National Committee of Bioethics, 2010), and the Personal Data Protection Law (Kingdom of Saudi Arabia, 2021). Informed consent, confidentiality, data protection and withdrawal procedures were applied as described in Sections 4.4 and 4.6.

The main foreseeable risks were professional and reputational, because managers might describe decisions that colleagues or employers could view critically, and because the population was small and identifiable. To reduce these risks:

- employers were not told who took part;
- sites, units and participants are described in reports only at an aggregate level;
- quotations are attributed only by participant and hospital code, and details that could identify a unit were removed or generalised; and
- no quotation is reported with a combination of characteristics that could identify an individual.

The consent information explained the limits of confidentiality. If a participant described an ongoing, unaddressed risk of serious harm to patients, the interviewer would encourage the participant to use established organisational reporting channels. No such disclosure occurred. Participants could pause or stop the interview at any time. Information about the hospital employee-support service was available to all participants, and no participant needed it.

## 4.11 Reporting Guideline

The study is reported in accordance with the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR; O'Brien et al., 2014). The completed SRQR checklist, together with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ; Tong et al., 2007), is provided as Supplementary File 2.
