## 5.2 Overview of the interpretive account

Managers did not treat informal GenAI use as a single type of behaviour that was simply acceptable or unacceptable. They judged whether intervention was warranted by tracing four things: what information had entered a tool, what had come out of it, where the output travelled and who had checked it. Where an explicit restriction applied, the rule largely made the decision. Where permission was unclear, managers filled the gap with professional judgement, personal accountability and borrowed authority, so their responses were more variable, more provisional and more likely to be revisited. Five themes, comprising fourteen subthemes, describe this reasoning (Table 4).

## 5.3 Theme 1: Making sense of what had happened

Before deciding how to respond, managers first had to establish what had happened. This was rarely straightforward, because their knowledge of an episode ranged from direct observation to an unconfirmed impression.

### 1.1 Coming to know: seeing, being told and suspecting

Episodes came to managers' attention through observation during rounds, reports from colleagues, physicians or families, disclosure by the nurse, or suspicion based on changes in written material. Each route offered a different level of certainty. Observed episodes left little doubt about what had happened:

> "I was doing my round and passed by the station. She had her phone next to the computer and was copying from the phone into the nursing note. When I looked, it was the chat program, and it was writing the care plan for her." (C-NM04, first-line manager; translated)

Suspicion, by contrast, created an evidential dilemma. Managers recognised features they associated with generated text, such as sudden fluency, a uniform structure and repeated stock phrases, but were reluctant to act on impressions alone:

> "Sometimes you just know. The English in the education leaflet was suddenly perfect, the same structure every time, the same phrases—'it is important to note.' This nurse does not write like that. But knowing is not proof, and I cannot accuse someone because her English improved." (D-NM05, first-line manager)

Nurse disclosure often took the form of a request for approval. Managers then found themselves asked to rule on something they had not previously considered:

> "She came to me herself and said, 'I used ChatGPT to make the discharge instructions simpler. Is that okay?' Honestly, I did not have an answer ready. Nobody had asked me this before." (B-NM04, first-line manager; translated)

### 1.2 Locating risk in what went in and what came out

Once aware of an episode, managers moved quickly from the general question of whether GenAI had been used to specific questions about its consequences. The first concern was usually what information had been entered, because a disclosure of patient information could not be reversed:

> "The first question in my head was not 'why did you use it?' It was 'what did you put inside?' If there is a name, a file number, a diagnosis with age and room, then the problem is already outside the hospital, and I cannot bring it back." (A-NM05, middle manager; translated)

The second concern was where the output went. Material that reached patients or the clinical record attracted more scrutiny than internal or administrative writing, because errors would travel with it:

> "A nurse using it to write an email to me, I don't mind. A nurse using it to explain insulin to a mother who will go home and give it to her child—this is different. The words go to the patient, so the mistake goes to the patient." (D-NM03, first-line manager)

Translation episodes showed this reasoning most clearly. In units where many nurses were not native Arabic speakers, managers recognised that generated translations could improve communication with families. They also recognised that the nurse could not verify text in a language they could not read:

> "With translation it is two sides. The family understood better than with our broken Arabic—I saw that. But who checked the Arabic? The nurse cannot read it. So the information is going to the family without any nurse being able to say it is correct." (A-NM04, first-line manager)

## 5.4 Theme 2: Calibrating whether and when to act

Managers' judgements about whether to intervene depended on whether a clear rule applied, how they classified uses that no rule addressed, the characteristics of the nurse and the task, and the timing of any response.

### 2.1 Clear lines: patient information as a firm threshold

Entering patient-identifiable information into an external tool was the one restriction that participants at every site recognised as explicit. In these episodes, managers described acting immediately and with little deliberation:

> "Here there is no discussion. Patient data outside the hospital system is forbidden; everyone signed the confidentiality form. I told her to stop immediately, delete the conversation in front of me, and I informed my director the same day." (B-NM03, middle manager; translated)

Several managers described the rule as relieving them of the need to judge seriousness for themselves:

> "For me it was easy, because the rule makes the decision. I don't need to think, is it serious or not serious. The policy already thought for me." (C-NM02, first-line manager; translated)

The threshold was less clear than it first appeared, however. Where nurses had removed names but entered other clinical details, managers were unsure whether the information remained identifiable, particularly in a regional setting where patients could be recognised from a few characteristics:

> "She said, 'I didn't write the name, only age, diagnosis and the lab results.' Is that identifiable? In a small city like ours, a young patient with this rare condition in this hospital—maybe people know who it is. I was not sure, and I don't think the policy is sure." (D-NM06, middle manager; translated)

### 2.2 Grey zones: judging use that nobody had permitted or prohibited

Most episodes involved uses that local policy neither permitted nor prohibited. In these situations, managers described carrying both the decision and its consequences personally:

> "There is nothing written that says yes or no. So the decision is mine, and if something happens, it is also mine. That is the heavy part." (A-NM03, first-line manager; translated)

To make these situations manageable, managers compared GenAI with familiar practices. The comparison they chose shaped their response. Managers who likened GenAI to searching the internet or consulting a drug reference tended to permit use with confirmation from an approved source:

> "I compared it to when nurses search on Google or look in the drug book. We never forbid that; we ask them to confirm with the pharmacist. So I treated it the same: you can look, but you confirm from an approved source." (C-NM01, first-line manager; translated)

Others explicitly rejected comparisons that would give the tool the standing of an accountable professional, and responded more restrictively:

> "Other managers say it is like a colleague giving advice. I say no—a colleague is accountable, has a licence. This program has no licence. If I accept it like a colleague, I accept something nobody approved." (B-NM06, first-line manager)

### 2.3 Weighing the nurse, the task and the moment

Within the same permission condition, managers' responses varied with who the nurse was. Experience and demonstrated competence were treated as safeguards that could catch errors in generated content:

> "With an experienced nurse who knows the medications, I trust she will catch the error. With a new graduate, she may copy the care plan without understanding. Same tool, different risk, because the person is different." (A-NM02, first-line manager)

Workload and timing also shaped responses. Managers recognised staffing pressure as a reason for use and often delayed conversations until they could be held calmly, rather than interrupting care:

> "It was night shift, we were two nurses short. She used it to finish the documentation faster. I understood why. I did not stop her in the middle of the shift; I spoke to her the next morning when it was calm." (C-NM06, middle manager; translated)

For newly qualified nurses, managers' concern extended beyond the immediate episode to the development of clinical reasoning:

> "My worry with the young nurses is not only today's mistake. If the program writes their care plans, when will they learn to think like a nurse?" (D-NM01, first-line manager; translated)

### 2.4 Choosing to wait or not to act

Decisions not to intervene, or to defer intervention, were deliberate rather than passive. For low-risk administrative and educational uses, some managers judged that visible restriction would drive use out of sight and remove their ability to oversee it:

> "If I make a big problem, they will continue but they will hide it. Now at least they tell me. So for the presentations and the emails I said nothing; I just keep my eye open." (B-NM02, first-line manager)

When managers suspected use but lacked evidence, they often chose closer monitoring over confrontation, designed so that no individual felt targeted:

> "I suspected, but I had nothing in my hand. So I did not talk to her directly. I started checking the notes of that shift more often—for everyone, not only her—so she would not feel targeted." (D-NM04, first-line manager; translated)

One participant offered a divergent account, viewing GenAI as a personal tool outside the scope of managerial oversight as long as the nurse took responsibility for the final content. Other participants did not share this view, but it showed that some managers placed responsibility entirely on the individual nurse:

> "Honestly, I did not see it as something for me to manage. It is a tool, like a calculator. The nurse is responsible for what she signs. If the note is correct, how she wrote it is her business." (C-NM05, first-line manager)

## 5.5 Theme 3: Acting at the edge of authority

Deciding that intervention was warranted did not settle whether managers felt entitled to intervene. Where policy was silent, managers questioned the legitimacy of their own decisions and looked for ways to share authority with others.

### 3.1 Uncertain mandate: forbidding what no one had forbidden

Managers were reluctant to restrict practices that their institution had not prohibited, because they could not point to a basis for formal action:

> "How can I write a warning for something the hospital never said is wrong? If she complains, what will I show? My opinion?" (A-NM06, first-line manager; translated)

The national emphasis on digital transformation added to this hesitation. Some managers feared that restriction would appear to oppose wider organisational priorities, and they acknowledged that the compromise instructions they gave lacked clarity:

> "Everywhere you hear digital transformation, Vision 2030, use technology. Then I come and say 'don't use it'—I look like I am against the vision. So I said 'use it carefully', which is not a clear instruction, I know." (B-NM07, middle manager; translated)

Staff also compared practices between units, which turned managerial decisions into questions of consistency and fairness:

> "My staff told me, 'In the other unit the head nurse allows it.' So now it is not about the tool; it is about why my rule is different from her rule." (B-NM01, first-line manager; translated)

### 3.2 Borrowing authority through consultation and escalation

Before formal escalation, managers commonly consulted trusted peers. Member reflections reinforced this pattern, with several respondents describing peer consultation as the usual first step in unclear situations:

> "Before I go to the director, I call two or three head nurses I trust: 'What would you do?' If they all say report it, I report it. If they say talk to her, I talk to her. We make our own small committee." (D-NM02, middle manager; translated)

Formal escalation often produced confirmation of what managers already knew rather than guidance on what to do. Managers at Site A described quicker access to an information-security contact. Managers at other sites more often described slow replies or requests routed through the regional directorate:

> "I sent an email to IT asking, 'Is this application allowed?' The answer came after three weeks: 'It is not an approved application.' That I knew. I wanted to know what to do." (B-NM05, first-line manager; translated)

Escalation also served to share accountability. Managers openly described it as protecting themselves as well as patients:

> "I escalated partly to protect the patient and partly, honestly, to protect myself. If it is written that I informed the director, then the decision is not only on my shoulders." (A-NM01, middle manager; translated)

## 5.6 Theme 4: Shaping meaning and revisiting judgement

Managers' responses did not end with a decision. They attended carefully to how decisions were communicated, extended lessons from individual episodes to their teams and, in some cases, later changed their views.

### 4.1 Correcting without blaming

Managers usually addressed episodes privately and aimed to help the nurse recognise the problem for themselves rather than simply be told about it:

> "I closed the door and asked her to show me how she used it. I did not start with 'you made a mistake.' I wanted her to understand herself that the patient's information went outside. When she saw it, she said, 'I didn't think about it like that.'" (A-NM03, first-line manager; translated)

Protecting the nurse's dignity was described as essential to correction having its intended effect:

> "In our culture, if you correct someone in front of others, she will remember the embarrassment, not the lesson. So I always do it alone and I say, 'I am protecting you, not punishing you.'" (B-NM04, first-line manager; translated)

Some managers reframed the issue from whether the tool had been used to whether the nurse could take professional responsibility for the content. This was a deliberate attempt to change how the nurse understood the episode:

> "I told him, 'The question is not whether you used it. The question is, can you defend every word in that note if the physician or the family asks you?'" (C-NM02, first-line manager; translated)

### 4.2 From one episode to a unit message

Managers often turned individual episodes into wider expectations for their teams. Messages that stated concrete rules were described as easier for staff to remember and follow:

> "After the second case I said it in the morning huddle, without names: no patient information in any outside application, and anything you give to the patient, you check with the approved reference. Two rules, simple, so everyone remembers." (B-NM07, middle manager; translated)

General messages, by contrast, were open to divergent interpretations, including interpretations that the manager had not intended:

> "I sent a message in our unit group: 'Use your clinical judgement with these new applications.' Later I realised everyone understood it differently. One nurse thought I had allowed it." (D-NM05, first-line manager)

Middle managers, who saw episodes across several units, more often treated recurring episodes as organisational issues and directed their communication to head nurses rather than to staff:

> "When I see the same thing in three units, it is not a nurse problem any more; it is a system problem. Then my message goes to all the head nurses, not to the staff." (C-NM03, middle manager)

### 4.3 Second thoughts: revising judgements over time

Managers revised their judgements in both directions. Some became more restrictive after discovering an error in content they had previously accepted:

> "At first I allowed the leaflets because they looked very professional. Then one leaflet said the patient can take the medication with grapefruit juice, which is wrong for that drug. We caught it before it was given to anyone. After that, nothing goes to a patient without the clinical pharmacist or the educator signing it." (A-NM02, first-line manager)

Others relaxed an initially strict position after realising that prohibition had reduced visibility rather than use:

> "I was very strict in the beginning. Then I discovered they were using it anyway, at home, and bringing the text in. My strictness did not stop the use; it only stopped me knowing about it. So I changed my approach." (D-NM01, first-line manager; translated)

For some managers, reconsideration remained unresolved:

> "I still don't know if I was right. Maybe I was too soft. I think about it whenever I sign the audit for that unit." (B-NM03, middle manager; translated)

## 5.7 Theme 5: Seeking a shared map for oversight

Managers described the support they needed to oversee informal use effectively. Their accounts focused less on prohibition than on clarity, shared responsibility and knowledge.

### 5.1 Specific guidance rather than blanket rules

Managers wanted guidance that distinguished between tools, tasks and types of information rather than a general prohibition:

> "We don't need a policy that says 'artificial intelligence is prohibited.' Nobody will follow it. We need a list: this is allowed for this, this is not allowed, this needs approval." (A-NM05, middle manager; translated)

An approved tool operating within institutional systems was seen as a way to replace difficult judgements with a simple instruction:

> "Give us one approved tool inside the hospital system, where the data stays inside. Then the question becomes easy: use this one, not the one on your phone." (C-NM01, first-line manager; translated)

Even brief, example-based guidance was expected to reduce the burden of deciding alone:

> "Even a one-page guide with examples—leaflet yes, with checking; patient notes no—would save me hours of thinking alone." (B-NM02, first-line manager)

### 5.2 Clear lines of verification and escalation

Managers described uncertainty about where responsibility lay if generated content caused harm, and felt that it currently fell on them by default:

> "If an error reaches the patient, who is responsible—the nurse, me, or the hospital that never gave guidance? Right now everybody points at the head nurse." (C-NM04, first-line manager; translated)

They called for defined escalation routes with timely responses:

> "I want to know the path: for privacy I call this person, for clinical content this person, and they answer within a day, not a month." (D-NM02, middle manager; translated)

They also wanted verification responsibilities written into procedures, particularly where nurses could not check the output themselves:

> "Verification should be written in the procedure: who checks the translated text, who checks the education material. Now it is the nurse alone, and she cannot check what she cannot read." (A-NM04, first-line manager)

### 5.3 Knowledge to judge, not only to forbid

Managers identified their own limited familiarity with GenAI as a barrier to judging risk:

> "I am supposed to supervise something I have never used properly myself. How can I judge the risk of a tool I don't understand?" (A-NM06, first-line manager; translated)

They wanted training that would enable them to guide staff rather than only enforce restrictions:

> "Training should not be only 'don't do this.' Show managers what these programs can do, what mistakes they make, how to check. Then we can guide the staff, not just police them." (B-NM06, first-line manager)

The participant who had viewed GenAI use as the nurse's own responsibility (Section 5.4) shared this emphasis on capability. Their reasoning was that verification skills would protect patients more reliably than rules:

> "Teach the nurses to check—that is the real safety. Rules alone will always be behind the technology." (C-NM05, first-line manager)

## 5.8 Variation across permission conditions, managerial levels and sites

Comparison across episodes showed that permission clarity changed the basis of managerial reasoning rather than simply its strictness (Table 5). Where an explicit restriction applied, managers referred to the rule, acted quickly and shared accountability with the institution. Where permission was unclear, they relied on analogies, personal judgement and peer consultation, took more time, chose a wider range of responses and carried accountability themselves. In the six episodes where both conditions applied, managers addressed the restriction first and considered the broader use afterwards.

**Table 5. Comparison of managerial reasoning by permission condition**

| Dimension | Explicit restriction relevant (n = 27 episodes) | Unclear permission relevant (n = 37 episodes) |
|---|---|---|
| Basis of judgement | Reference to an existing rule | Professional judgement, analogies with familiar practices, peer views |
| Speed of response | Usually immediate | Often deliberative or deferred |
| Typical responses | Restriction, deletion of the conversation, escalation | Guidance, additional checking, closer monitoring, no intervention |
| Locus of accountability | Shared with the institution through the rule | Largely carried by the individual manager |
| Communication | Rule-referencing; brief | Explanatory; framed around professional responsibility |
| Residual uncertainty | What counted as identifiable information | Whether the use was acceptable at all, and who could decide |

*Six episodes involved both conditions and are included in both columns.*

Managerial level also shaped responses. First-line managers more often responded through individual conversations and unit-level monitoring. Middle managers more often treated recurring episodes as organisational issues, escalated them and directed communication to head nurses. Across sites, the main difference concerned access to expertise: managers at Site A described a reachable information-security contact, whereas managers at Sites B, C and D relied more on peer consultation and regional channels. Not every account fitted the sensitising concepts. Deferrals driven by workload, and the view that GenAI use lay outside managerial oversight (Section 5.4), involved little apparent reinterpretation of the situation or effort to shape others' understanding. These accounts were retained as part of the range of managerial reasoning.
