# 2. Methods

*Whose Readiness Counts? Women's, Maternity Nurses' and Physiotherapists' Accounts of Mobilisation Decisions After Emergency Caesarean Birth*

---

## 2.1 Research design and methodological orientation

This study used Interpretive Description, a qualitative approach developed within nursing to produce contextually grounded knowledge that can inform clinical practice (Thorne, 2025). Interpretive Description suited the research question for three reasons. First, the phenomenon is a practice problem: decisions about when and how a woman moves after emergency caesarean birth happen in routine postnatal care, under clinical and organisational constraints. Second, the approach asks the analyst to go beyond describing experiences and interpret patterns that clinicians can recognise and use. Third, it allows existing disciplinary knowledge, including the nursing and physiotherapy evidence summarised in the Introduction, to frame the inquiry. That knowledge serves as a scaffold the data can question, not as a fixed theory.

The study was underpinned by a constructivist–interpretive position. Participants' accounts were treated as meaningful, situated explanations of how decisions were reasoned about, communicated and revised. They were not treated as objective records of what happened at the bedside. Accordingly, the analysis interpreted *reported* decision-making. It did not adjudicate the clinical appropriateness of particular encounters.

Relational autonomy (Entwistle et al., 2010; Stoljar and Mackenzie, 2023) and the orientation shown in Figure 1 (Section 1.1) acted as sensitising concepts. They shaped areas of enquiry in the interview guides: what information women received, opportunities to express preferences, requests for assistance or reassessment, and the clinical and resource constraints on participation. They did not provide a coding frame, predefined categories or expected findings. Codes were developed inductively from the data. During analysis, the distinctions in Figure 1 were deliberately tested against the data: between readiness appraisals, reported decisions and reconsideration. They would have been revised had the accounts not supported them (Section 2.6).

**Phenomenon and boundaries.** The phenomenon was participants' reported decisions about first assisted mobilisation and subsequent progression during the initial postoperative hospital admission. The activities covered were changing position in bed, sitting on the edge of the bed, transferring out of bed, standing and walking. Readiness appraisals were defined as judgements about a woman's current capacity, clinically assessed and perceived safety, willingness and support requirements for a specified activity. Decisions were defined as reported actions to attempt, assist, adapt, pause or defer that activity. Reconsideration was defined as any reported revision of an earlier appraisal or decision. Mobilisation after discharge, and decisions about postoperative care unrelated to movement, fell outside the boundaries of the study except where participants linked them to in-hospital mobilisation.

**Reporting.** The study is reported according to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ; Tong et al., 2007), which applies to the whole report. The completed checklist, with manuscript locations, is provided as Supplementary File S2.

## 2.2 Study setting and mobilisation care pathways

**National context.** In Saudi Arabia, public healthcare is being reorganised into regional health clusters under the Health Holding Company as part of the Health Sector Transformation Program (Health Holding Company, n.d.). Private hospitals operate alongside the cluster system under Ministry of Health licensing. Nurses, midwives and physiotherapists are separately classified and registered by the Saudi Commission for Health Specialties (SCFHS). Postnatal wards in many Saudi hospitals, including the participating hospitals, are staffed mainly by registered nurses working in maternity care. Midwives mainly practise in labour and delivery units. Because of this division of labour, the study focused on maternity nurses as the professionals providing routine postoperative mobilisation support. Midwives were not recruited as a separate group.

**Participating hospitals.** The study was conducted in Al-Jouf Region, northern Saudi Arabia, at three hospitals:

- **Hospital A** and **Hospital B** were government hospitals within Al-Jouf Health Cluster.
- **Hospital C** was a private regional hospital outside the cluster.

All three provided obstetric surgery and had dedicated postnatal wards. After emergency caesarean birth, women were transferred from the operating theatre to a post-anaesthesia care unit. When they met the anaesthetic discharge criteria, they were transferred to the postnatal ward, where the first assisted mobilisation and subsequent progression took place. Women requiring high-dependency or intensive care were cared for elsewhere and were outside the study.

**Mobilisation pathways.** The three hospitals shared a common basic pathway:

1. Maternity nurses assessed women before the first assisted mobilisation. Assessment covered vital signs, pain, sensory and motor recovery after neuraxial anaesthesia, dizziness, bleeding, and the presence of urinary catheters, drains and infusions.
2. Nurses initiated and assisted the first mobilisation, usually in stages: sitting up, sitting on the bed edge, standing, then walking.
3. Nurses adjusted later assistance according to the woman's symptoms and support needs, and reassessed her when she reported new or worsening symptoms.
4. Physiotherapy was referral-based. When nurses identified rehabilitation needs beyond routine assistance, they reported them to the obstetric team, which initiated a referral. Examples included persistent inability to transfer, marked fear of movement and pre-existing musculoskeletal conditions.
5. Physiotherapists assessed functional movement, taught transfer and walking strategies, and returned recommendations to the maternity team in the medical record and verbally to the nurse in charge.

**Service differences.** Hospitals A and B worked under the overarching cluster framework, but each documented its own ward-level procedures. Hospital C followed its own hospital pathway. Table 1 compares the sites on the dimensions examined: who provided assistance, referral criteria, who initiated referrals, physiotherapy availability and reassessment arrangements. The most consequential difference concerned physiotherapy availability outside routine hours:

- At Hospitals A and B, physiotherapists were available for postnatal referrals on weekday day shifts. Referrals made in the evening, at night or at weekends were seen on the next working day.
- At Hospital C, a physiotherapist was available for six days a week. Outside these hours, physiotherapy advice was available by telephone.

At all sites, nurses remained responsible for mobilisation assistance outside physiotherapy hours.

**Sources of pathway information.** Pathway descriptions were compiled from three sources: written ward policies and mobilisation or enhanced recovery protocols supplied by each hospital; a structured site-description form completed with each postnatal ward's nurse manager and the physiotherapy department lead before data collection began; and field notes recorded during recruitment visits. Where a written protocol and reported practice differed, both are described. Interpretation relied on participants' accounts of what happened in practice.

## 2.3 Participants, eligibility, sampling and recruitment

### 2.3.1 Definition of emergency caesarean birth

Emergency caesarean birth was defined as any caesarean birth not scheduled in advance. This covered unplanned procedures decided upon after labour had begun, or because of maternal or fetal compromise, and corresponds to urgency categories 1 to 3 of the classification by Lucas et al. (2000). Planned (category 4) caesarean births were excluded, including planned procedures brought forward because labour began spontaneously. The urgency category was confirmed from the operative record.

### 2.3.2 Eligibility criteria

**Women** were eligible if they:

- were aged 18 years or older;
- had given birth by emergency caesarean under neuraxial or general anaesthesia at a participating hospital;
- had experienced at least one assisted mobilisation episode during the initial postoperative admission;
- could take part in an interview in Arabic or English.

Women were excluded if:

- they or their infant were seriously unwell (for example, admission to intensive care, or neonatal death or critical illness), because an interview would be an unacceptable burden during acute distress;
- they lacked capacity to give informed consent.

Women whose mobilisation was delayed, declined, paused or deferred were deliberately **not** excluded, because such decisions are central to the inquiry. Fitness to be interviewed was assessed separately from readiness to mobilise. A woman could be unable or unwilling to walk yet entirely fit to describe how that decision was reached. Before every interview, the nurse caring for the woman confirmed that she was alert, sufficiently comfortable and not recently sedated. The woman also confirmed that she felt able to talk.

**Maternity nurses** were eligible if they:

- held current SCFHS registration as nurses;
- had at least six months' experience on a postnatal ward at a participating hospital;
- had personally assessed or assisted women's mobilisation after emergency caesarean birth within the preceding three months.

Nurses in supervisory roles were eligible if they continued to provide direct care. Student and agency staff were excluded because they lacked sustained involvement in the ward's mobilisation practice.

**Physiotherapists** were eligible if they:

- held current SCFHS registration as physiotherapists;
- had assessed or treated at least one woman after caesarean birth at a participating hospital within the preceding six months.

No minimum years of experience was required, because the number of physiotherapists receiving postnatal referrals was small.

### 2.3.3 Sampling strategy

Purposive sampling with maximum variation was used within each group (Palinkas et al., 2015). Variation sought:

- **Women:** hospital; parity (first birth or subsequent); urgency category; type of anaesthesia; whether labour preceded the caesarean; timing of first mobilisation (within or beyond 24 hours); whether progression was straightforward or included pausing, deferral or physiotherapy referral; infant co-location (rooming-in or neonatal unit).
- **Nurses:** hospital; years of postnatal experience; shift pattern; nationality and first language, reflecting the internationally recruited nursing workforce.
- **Physiotherapists:** hospital; years of experience; whether women's health formed a regular part of their caseload.

As analysis progressed, recruitment was guided towards under-represented episode types. Women who had declined or deferred mobilisation were actively sought, as were professionals with experience of referral.

### 2.3.4 Recruitment

At each hospital, the postnatal ward nurse manager acted as gatekeeper for women and nurses, and the physiotherapy department lead for physiotherapists. Gatekeepers circulated information but did not approach individuals or know who took part.

**Women.** The nurse in charge identified potentially eligible women from the ward census and asked whether they were willing to hear about the study. With permission, the interviewing researcher (MAA) gave verbal and written information in Arabic or English and allowed at least 12 hours before seeking consent.

**Professionals.** Nurses and physiotherapists received an information sheet through ward and departmental communication channels and contacted the researchers directly if they were interested.

**Safeguards against pressure.** The following steps were taken:

- No researcher held a clinical, managerial or supervisory role at any participating hospital.
- Professionals were not recruited through line managers' requests.
- Women's care providers were not told whether they had agreed to take part.
- All participants were assured, verbally and in writing, that declining would not affect their care or employment.

### 2.3.5 Linked and independent accounts

Accounts were independently described, not linked. Women, nurses and physiotherapists were recruited separately. No attempt was made to recruit the professionals involved in a particular woman's care, and professionals described their own experience of mobilisation episodes in general and through specific de-identified examples. This design protected confidentiality on small wards and avoided placing women and their caregivers in an evaluative relationship. It also means that cross-group comparison shows contrasting or converging patterns of reasoning, not corroboration of shared encounters (Section 2.6).

### 2.3.6 Sample size and sample adequacy

**Planned sample size.** Interpretive Description does not prescribe a sample size; Thorne (2025) advises that adequacy depends on the analytic purpose and the variation needed to support credible, clinically useful interpretation. The planned ranges were therefore informed by three bodies of evidence:

- A systematic review of empirical saturation studies found that 9 to 17 interviews generally reached saturation in relatively homogeneous groups with focused objectives (Hennink and Kaiser, 2022).
- Code saturation, where the range of issues is identified, may be reached at around 9 interviews. Meaning saturation, where those issues are richly understood, required 16 to 24 interviews (Hennink et al., 2017).
- Information power: the more relevant information a sample holds, the fewer participants are needed. It depends on study aim, sample specificity, use of established theory, quality of dialogue and analysis strategy (Malterud et al., 2016).

These considerations supported different targets for each group:

- **Women (16 to 20).** Women's accounts carried the greatest variation in clinical course, symptoms and newborn-care demands. They also needed to cover all three hospitals and episodes of delayed and deferred mobilisation. The meaning-saturation range was therefore appropriate.
- **Maternity nurses (10 to 14).** Nurses formed a more homogeneous professional group with a focused role. Each nurse could also draw on many mobilisation episodes, which increases the information held per participant.
- **Physiotherapists (6 to 8).** The eligible population was small because physiotherapy was referral-based. Each physiotherapist held specialised and highly specific information. Information power supported a smaller group, provided all three hospitals were represented.

**Judging adequacy during the study.** Saturation was not used as a stopping rule, because it does not fit interpretive analysis well (Braun and Clarke, 2021). Instead, data collection ended when the research team judged, in documented analytic meetings, that three conditions were met:

1. The data contained enough concrete decision episodes across activities (sitting, transferring, standing, walking) and recovery stages to support interpretation of how appraisals informed decisions and their reconsideration.
2. Each emerging interpretation was supported by accounts from more than one hospital and, where relevant, more than one participant group.
3. Recent interviews were refining existing interpretations rather than raising new aspects of decision reasoning.

## 2.4 Data collection and interview guide development

**Dates, mode and timing.** Data were collected between March and September 2026 through individual semi-structured interviews.

- **Women** were offered an interview in a private room on the postnatal ward from postoperative day 2 onwards, once fitness to be interviewed was confirmed. Alternatively, they could choose a telephone or video interview within 14 days of birth. Fourteen women were interviewed in hospital and four by telephone after discharge. In-hospital interviews were scheduled around feeding, observations and visiting, and were held after the woman's first assisted mobilisation, so that at least one episode could be described.
- **Professionals** chose a face-to-face interview in a private room away from the clinical area outside working hours, or a video interview.

Interviews were conducted in Arabic or English according to each participant's preference. Women's interviews lasted 28 to 55 minutes (median 39). Professionals' interviews lasted 41 to 74 minutes (median 52).

**Interviewers.** MAA conducted all interviews with women. To reduce the influence of shared professional assumptions, interviews with professionals were conducted across disciplines: SOA, a physiotherapist, interviewed the maternity nurses, and MAA, a nurse, interviewed the physiotherapists. Both researchers are women, which was culturally appropriate for interviewing postnatal women in this setting. Their backgrounds are described in Section 2.7.

**Guide development.** Three group-specific guides were developed from:

- the study objectives;
- the sensitising concepts (Section 1.1);
- the literature on mobilisation after caesarean birth and on negotiated mobilisation (Mørch et al., 2019; Lehmkuhl et al., 2024; Thörn et al., 2025);
- input from the patient and public involvement group (Section 2.9).

The guides shared a common structure so that accounts could be compared across groups. Each was adapted to the group's perspective:

- **Women's guide:** bodily experience, information received, preferences and how they were expressed, and newborn-care demands.
- **Nurses' guide:** assessment, assisting and adapting movement, workload, and reporting needs to the obstetric team.
- **Physiotherapists' guide:** referral pathways, functional assessment, and how recommendations were communicated and taken up.

The guides were written in English, translated into Arabic by MAA, and checked for conceptual equivalence by SOA. Colloquial Arabic terms for movement replaced the technical term for mobilisation. Examples were "getting up", "sitting on the edge of the bed" and "taking your first steps".

**Piloting and changes during data collection.** The guides were piloted with one woman and one maternity nurse who met the eligibility criteria. Piloting led to two changes. First, the opening question for women was moved from a general question about recovery to a concrete question about the first time they got out of bed, because the general question produced long accounts of the birth itself. Second, prompts about pain medication timing were added. Because the changes were minor, the pilot interviews were included in the analysis.

During data collection, the guides were refined iteratively, consistent with Interpretive Description. Examples:

- After early analysis showed the importance of night-time episodes, a prompt was added about mobilisation at night and outside physiotherapy hours.
- A prompt was added for professionals about situations in which a woman's and a professional's readiness appraisals differed.

All versions of the guides are dated and retained in the audit trail.

**Eliciting decision episodes.** Each interview centred on concrete episodes. Participants were asked to describe a specific occasion, such as the first time the woman sat up, stood or walked, or a recent occasion when a professional helped a woman move. For each episode, prompts established:

- the proposed activity;
- the woman's symptoms and concerns at the time;
- who contributed to the judgement of readiness;
- what was said and by whom;
- what reportedly happened;
- why the activity went ahead, was adapted, paused or deferred;
- what, if anything, later prompted reconsideration.

Participants were explicitly invited to describe routine and straightforward episodes and episodes of agreement, not only difficult encounters. Reconsideration was explored retrospectively within the same interview, so repeat interviews were not routinely required.

**Recording, field notes and interruptions.** All interviews were audio-recorded with consent. Immediately afterwards, the interviewer wrote field notes covering the setting, non-verbal communication, interruptions and initial analytic impressions. Seven in-hospital interviews with women were paused briefly for infant feeding or clinical care and then resumed with the participant's agreement. No repeat interviews were needed.

**Companions.** Women were offered the choice of being interviewed alone. In five interviews, a female relative remained present at the woman's request. The presence of companions, and any contributions they made, were recorded in field notes and considered during analysis. Only the woman's own words were quoted.

**Contextual information.** A brief contextual form was completed for each participant (Supplementary File S1):

- **Women:** age band, parity, urgency category, anaesthesia type, whether labour preceded the caesarean, postoperative day at interview, timing of first mobilisation, whether physiotherapy was received, and infant location. Clinical items were taken from the medical record with consent.
- **Professionals:** role, years of experience, usual shift pattern, and approximate frequency of involvement in post-caesarean mobilisation.

**Supplementary material.** The complete interview guides for all three groups and the contextual information form are provided in Supplementary File S1.

## 2.5 Transcription, translation and data management

**Transcription.** Recordings were transcribed verbatim in the language of the interview by the interviewing researcher, or by a bilingual research assistant who had signed a confidentiality agreement. Transcripts included pauses, hesitations and laughter where these bore on meaning. Every transcript was checked against its recording by the interviewer, and errors were corrected.

**De-identification and coding.** During transcription, names, hospital names, ward names, staff names, dates and other potentially identifying details were removed or replaced. Each participant was assigned a code indicating group and sequence: W01–W18 for women, N01–N12 for nurses and P01–P07 for physiotherapists. Hospital was recorded separately in a restricted linkage file. Quotations are not attributed to hospital, because the small number of physiotherapists at each site could make individuals identifiable. Where a participant described a recognisable incident, non-essential details were altered or omitted, without changing meaning.

**Languages.** Twenty-seven interviews were conducted in Arabic: all 18 women, 4 nurses and 5 physiotherapists. Ten were conducted in English: 8 nurses and 2 physiotherapists. Arabic transcripts were coded in Arabic by the two Arabic-speaking researchers, so that analysis stayed close to participants' original expressions (van Nes et al., 2010). Analytic memos, team discussions and code labels were written in English, with the original Arabic terms recorded alongside where meaning depended on them.

**Translation.** Whole transcripts were not translated. Only quotations selected for publication were translated from Arabic into English, following the recommendations of Squires (2009) for cross-language qualitative research:

1. MAA, who is bilingual and works in English-medium nursing education, translated each quotation for meaning rather than word for word.
2. SOA independently compared each translation with the Arabic original.
3. Discrepancies were discussed until agreement was reached.
4. Culturally specific expressions with no direct English equivalent were translated by meaning, with a transliteration or brief explanation added where necessary. Examples included religious expressions of acceptance and colloquial terms for weakness or dizziness.

**Data storage.** Data were managed as follows:

- Audio files were transferred from the encrypted recorder to a password-protected, encrypted Jouf University server on the day of the interview, and then deleted from the recorder.
- Consent forms and the linkage file were stored separately from the transcripts, in a locked cabinet and an encrypted folder respectively.
- Only the two researchers had access to identifiable data. The research assistant accessed only the audio files assigned for transcription.
- De-identified transcripts, analytic files and the audit trail will be retained for five years after publication, in accordance with institutional policy, and then securely destroyed.
- Audio recordings will be destroyed once the analysis is published.

## 2.6 Data analysis

**Analytic approach.** Analysis followed the inductive, iterative logic of Interpretive Description (Thorne, 2025). The aim was to construct a coherent interpretive account of how readiness appraisals informed mobilisation decisions and their reconsideration, rather than to produce a list of barriers, facilitators or experiences. Throughout, analysis was guided by Thorne's questions: "What is happening here?", "What am I learning about this?" and "What might this mean for practice?"

**Concurrent collection and analysis.** Analysis began after the first interviews and proceeded alongside data collection. Early interpretations shaped later sampling and guide refinements (Sections 2.3.3 and 2.4). Examples include actively seeking accounts of deferred mobilisation, and adding prompts about night-time episodes and differing appraisals.

**Familiarisation and coding.** Each transcript was read in full several times alongside its field notes before coding began, so that each account was understood as a whole. Broad-based, inductive coding was then applied line by line to segments describing appraisals, actions, communication, reasons and context. Thorne (2025) warns against premature fragmentation, so codes were kept close to participants' language in the early stages and were grouped only once relationships between them became apparent. Analytic memos were written throughout. They recorded emerging ideas, questions, links between accounts and reflections on the researchers' own reactions.

**Episode-level analysis.** To distinguish readiness appraisals from reported actions and from reasons for reconsideration, every concrete decision episode was summarised in an episode matrix. Each row was one episode, and the columns recorded:

- the activity (positioning, sitting, transferring, standing or walking);
- the recovery stage (first mobilisation or later progression);
- the readiness appraisals reported, and who made them (woman, nurse, physiotherapist or family member);
- the information and concerns informing each appraisal;
- the reported action (attempt, assist, adapt, pause or defer);
- the reasons given for that action;
- any reconsideration and what prompted it.

This structure kept the analytic boundary clear between *what was judged* and *what was done*. It also allowed analysts to see where the same appraisal led to different actions, or where actions followed one person's appraisal rather than another's. A total of 164 episodes were summarised: 71 from women, 63 from nurses and 30 from physiotherapists.

**Comparisons.** Constant comparison was applied at three levels:

1. **Within accounts:** how a participant's reasoning changed across successive episodes or activities. For example, a woman ready to sit but not to stand.
2. **Within groups:** variation in reasoning among women, among nurses and among physiotherapists, including by hospital, experience, parity and clinical course where the data supported it.
3. **Across groups:** how women, nurses and physiotherapists explained the weight given to different appraisals, and how they described communication, participation and responsibility.

Because accounts were independently recruited (Section 2.3.5), cross-group comparisons were interpreted as contrasting or converging *patterns* of reasoning. They were not treated as competing versions of the same encounter. Attention to activity, recovery stage and service context was retained where the data supported such distinctions. For example, episodes occurring outside physiotherapy hours were compared with daytime episodes.

**Developing and refining interpretations.** Codes were grouped into provisional patterns, which were then examined against the full data set and the episode matrix. Interpretations were refined through repeated movement between the parts (codes and episodes) and the whole (complete accounts and the overall data set). Analysis deliberately sought:

- **Differing and disconfirming accounts:** episodes in which appraisals agreed, routine episodes, and accounts that did not fit emerging patterns. These were used to qualify or revise interpretations.
- **Alternative explanations:** for each major interpretation, the team asked whether another explanation could equally account for the data. Examples were workload or staffing rather than clinical judgement, and family preference rather than the woman's own appraisal.
- **Challenges to the initial framework:** the distinctions in Figure 1 were tested, not assumed. For example, the analysis examined whether appraisals and decisions could in practice be separated in participants' accounts, and whether reconsideration followed from new information or from changes in who was present.

**Analysts and software.** MAA and SOA both coded all transcripts, Arabic and English. Coding was not used to calculate agreement. Instead, each analyst's coding and memos were compared in analytic meetings to broaden interpretation, drawing on their complementary nursing and physiotherapy perspectives. Differences were treated as prompts for discussion and re-examination of the data, not as errors to resolve. Data were organised in NVivo 14 (Lumivero). The episode matrix was maintained in a spreadsheet linked to transcript locations.

## 2.7 Researcher reflexivity and methodological rigour

### 2.7.1 Researcher reflexivity

**Backgrounds.** MAA is a nurse and faculty member in maternal and child health nursing. She has clinical experience in maternity care and has conducted qualitative interviews with women during the perinatal period. SOA is a physiotherapist and faculty member in physiotherapy, with clinical experience of musculoskeletal and women's health rehabilitation. Both researchers have training in qualitative research methods. MAA has previous experience with Interpretive Description. Both are Saudi women who speak Arabic as their first language and are fluent in English.

**Relationships with participants and services.** Neither researcher was employed by, or held a clinical, supervisory or managerial role at, any participating hospital during the study. Some nurses and physiotherapists may have been former students of Jouf University. Interviews with professionals were allocated across disciplines (Section 2.4), so no professional was interviewed by a researcher who had taught them. Participants were told that the researchers were university faculty members conducting independent research, and that they were not evaluating individual practice or reporting to hospital management.

**What participants knew.** Participants were told the researchers' names, professions and university affiliation, and that the study aimed to understand how decisions about getting up and moving after emergency caesarean birth were made and explained. Women were told that the interviewer was a nurse but was not part of their care team.

**Assumptions and how they were examined.** Before data collection, each researcher wrote a reflexive statement of her assumptions:

- MAA anticipated that early mobilisation was generally beneficial and that women's reluctance often reflected fear that information could address.
- SOA anticipated that physiotherapy input was under-used and that nurses' workload constrained progression.
- Both expected that women's preferences might be given limited weight.

These statements were revisited at each analytic meeting and at the end of analysis. Each researcher kept a reflexive journal recording reactions to interviews and moments where data challenged expectations (Olmos-Vega et al., 2023). Cross-disciplinary interviewing and joint analysis were used deliberately so that each researcher's professional assumptions were questioned by a colleague from the other discipline.

These procedures changed the analysis in documented ways. Several women described reluctance as a reasoned judgement about dizziness or bleeding rather than fear. This led the team to stop using "fear" as an umbrella code. Accounts of nurses initiating physiotherapy referrals informally qualified the initial assumption that physiotherapy was under-used.

### 2.7.2 Methodological rigour

Rigour was guided by Thorne's (2025) criteria for Interpretive Description: epistemological integrity, representative credibility, analytic logic and interpretive authority. It was supported by the following procedures.

- **Epistemological integrity.** The research question, constructivist–interpretive position, interview approach and analysis were aligned. The analysis interpreted reported reasoning and made no claims about what happened objectively or whether care was clinically appropriate.
- **Representative credibility.** Interpretations were developed from variation across three hospitals, three participant groups and a range of clinical courses. Claims were limited to what the depth and breadth of the data supported. Patterns found in only one group or one hospital are reported as such.
- **Analytic logic.** An audit trail documented every analytic decision: dated versions of the interview guides; field notes; coded transcripts; the episode matrix; analytic memos; minutes of 11 analytic meetings; and successive versions of the interpretive structure. Readers can follow how interpretations were derived from the data.
- **Interpretive authority.** Competing explanations were actively considered and recorded in memos. Reflexive journals and cross-disciplinary discussion (Section 2.7.1) helped distinguish participants' accounts from the researchers' expectations.
- **Contextual detail and quotations.** The setting, pathways and service differences (Section 2.2) and aggregated participant characteristics are reported in detail, so that readers can judge transferability to their own services. Quotations from across the three groups and hospitals illustrate each interpretation. They were chosen to show both typical and contrasting accounts, not only the most vivid.
- **External review.** Preliminary interpretations were presented to an experienced qualitative researcher in nursing who was not involved in the study. She reviewed the audit trail and a sample of coded transcripts and challenged the interpretations. Her questions led the team to sharpen the distinction between who *made* a readiness appraisal and whose appraisal *determined* the action. Preliminary findings were also discussed with the patient and public involvement group (Section 2.9). This was a check on the relevance and clarity of the interpretations for women, not member checking. Participants themselves were not asked to validate the findings, because interpretations synthesised across many accounts would not map onto any individual's experience (Thorne, 2025).

## 2.8 Ethical considerations

**Approval.** The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of King Abdulaziz Specialist Hospital, Al-Jouf Health Cluster (committee registration H-13-S-161; approval No. 9804; 15 February 2026). This approval covered Hospitals A and B. Hospital C, which lies outside the cluster, granted separate administrative permission through its research committee before recruitment began there. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the Saudi National Committee of Bioethics regulations for research involving human subjects.

**Consent.** All participants gave written informed consent. The consent form specifically covered:

- participation in the interview;
- audio recording;
- use of de-identified quotations in publications;
- for women only, access to specified items from the medical record.

For telephone and video interviews, consent was obtained in writing before the interview and confirmed verbally at the start of the recording. Women were approached for consent separately from, and after, the initial information visit (Section 2.3.4). Professionals gave consent after contacting the researchers themselves.

**Voluntariness and withdrawal.** Participants were told that taking part was voluntary, that they could decline any question, and that they could stop the interview at any time. They could withdraw their data without giving a reason for up to 30 days after the interview. After that point, their de-identified data would have been combined with other data in the analysis, and removing it could no longer be guaranteed. This limit was explained before consent. No participant withdrew.

The following protections were in place for care and employment relationships:

- Women were assured that declining or withdrawing would not affect their care.
- Professionals were assured that participation and their accounts would not be disclosed to managers or used to evaluate their performance.
- No participant's account was shared with any other participant or with hospital staff.

**Pain, fatigue and distress.** Before each in-hospital interview, the woman's nurse confirmed that she was fit to be interviewed (Section 2.3.2). During the interview, the interviewer watched for signs of pain, fatigue or distress and offered breaks. Women could pause for feeding, care or rest and resume later or on another occasion. The interview was stopped if a woman asked or appeared unwell.

A distress protocol set out the following steps:

1. Pause and check how the woman was feeling.
2. Offer to stop the interview.
3. With her agreement, inform her nurse of any clinical concern, such as new pain or dizziness.
4. Signpost hospital psychological and social support services where relevant.

Two women became tearful when describing the emergency birth. Both chose to continue after a pause. One accepted information about the hospital's support services. Professionals who described distressing clinical events were offered time to pause and were directed to staff support services.

**Privacy.** In-hospital interviews were held in a private room or, where women could not leave their bed, behind closed curtains at a time when the bay was quiet. When a woman chose to have a companion present (Section 2.4), she was reminded beforehand that the companion would hear her answers and that she could ask to speak privately at any point. Companions were asked to keep the discussion confidential. Professionals' accounts were protected by:

- removing role-specific and site-specific details from quotations;
- not attributing quotations to hospital;
- reporting physiotherapists' characteristics only in aggregate.

**Linkage across participants.** Accounts were not linked across participants (Section 2.3.5). No consent for cross-participant linkage was therefore needed, and no participant was informed about another's participation or account.

## 2.9 Patient and public involvement

Patient and public involvement was planned and reported with reference to the GRIPP2 short-form checklist (Staniszewska et al., 2017). Four women with previous experience of emergency caesarean birth in Al-Jouf Region formed an advisory group. They were recruited through a university-affiliated community maternal health programme and were not study participants. They were offered reimbursement for their time and travel.

The group contributed at three stages:

1. **Design (before ethics submission).** In one meeting, the group reviewed the research question, the information sheet and consent form for women, and the draft women's interview guide. Three changes followed:
   - technical terms for mobilisation were replaced with everyday Arabic expressions;
   - a prompt was added about how caring for and feeding the newborn affected decisions to move;
   - the group advised that women should be able to choose interview timing around feeds and visiting, and should be offered the option of a telephone interview after discharge, which was adopted.
2. **Interpretation (during analysis).** In a second meeting, the group discussed anonymised preliminary interpretations. They confirmed that the distinction between feeling able to sit and feeling able to stand matched their own experience. They also highlighted the role of female relatives in encouraging or discouraging movement, which prompted closer analytic attention to companions' contributions.
3. **Dissemination (planned).** The group is helping to produce a plain-language Arabic summary of the findings for women and maternity services.

No public contributors were involved in conducting interviews or coding data.
