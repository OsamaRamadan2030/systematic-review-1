@TYPE Systematic Review
@TITLE Assisted Performance or Unaided Learning? A Systematic Review of Speech and Braille Technologies for School-Age Learners with Disabilities
@ABSTRACT Speech and Braille technologies are widely provided to school-age learners with disabilities, but better performance while a tool is in use does not show that a skill has been learned. This systematic review examined learner-level outcomes of speech-to-text (STT), text-to-speech (TTS), automatic captioning, adaptive Braille tutoring, and optical Braille recognition compared with usual instruction, an alternative accommodation, or baseline. PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and APA PsycInfo were searched in July 2026 and supplemented by ERIC, citation, and targeted searches in October 2026 (publication cutoff 23 July 2026). Narrative synthesis followed SWiM guidance and classified outcomes as assisted, unaided, or proximal. Forty-seven reports were included (32 TTS, 13 STT, one combined TTS/STT, one Braille tutor; 23 single-case designs). STT often increased written output, but accuracy and quality varied. TTS comprehension findings ranged from favorable to null or favoring the comparator. Controlled evidence of unaided learning was inconsistent, and one randomized experiment found poorer delayed orthographic learning after TTS than after independent reading. No eligible captioning or optical Braille recognition comparison was found. Small samples, multicomponent interventions, overlapping cohorts, and single-reviewer processes limit confidence. Educational teams should set and monitor access and learning goals separately. PROSPERO: CRD420261513927.
@KEYWORDS assistive technology; text-to-speech; speech-to-text; Braille; dyslexia; learning disabilities; reading comprehension; written expression; accommodations; systematic review

# 1. Introduction

An estimated 240 million children worldwide live with disabilities (United Nations Children’s Fund, 2021). Access to written and spoken language is central to their participation in education, and international frameworks recognize accessible communication and assistive technology as conditions for that participation (United Nations, 2006; World Health Assembly, 2018; World Health Organization & United Nations Children’s Fund, 2022). The International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health distinguishes activities, participation, and environmental facilitators (World Health Organization, n.d.). Applied to educational technology, the distinction directs attention to what a learner can understand and produce, not only to how well a device performs. Decisions about provision and follow-up involve teachers, families, speech-language and rehabilitation professionals, and school health services, and availability alone does not ensure continued use (Council on School Health, 2016; Federici & Borsci, 2016; World Health Organization & United Nations Children’s Fund, 2022).

Several technologies address different language-access barriers. Speech-to-text (STT) converts dictation into written language, and text-to-speech (TTS) converts written language into synthetic speech (Matre & Cameron, 2024; Wood et al., 2018). Automatic captioning displays spoken language as text. Adaptive Braille tutors provide performance-responsive practice and feedback, and optical Braille recognition (OBR) converts images of embossed Braille into an accessible representation (Asfaw et al., 2024; Kausar et al., 2021; Kushalnagar et al., 2014; Latif et al., 2023; McCarthy et al., 2016). Technical accuracy or feasibility does not establish comprehension, writing quality, or classroom participation; captioning research illustrates why learner-centered evaluation is needed (Millett, 2021–2022).

The central interpretive problem is that the same technology can be judged by different questions. A tool may compensate for a barrier while it is in use (an access, or accommodation, effect), it may support practice that changes a targeted skill (a proximal acquisition effect), or it may lead to better performance once it is removed (an unaided learning, or transfer, effect). These outcomes have different implications. An accommodation can be valuable even if unaided skill does not change, whereas an instructional claim requires evidence obtained without the tool. Reviews that combine these outcomes can overstate instructional effects and can also obscure genuine access benefits.

Previous reviews have examined TTS and related read-aloud tools, STT for adolescents, technology for Braille literacy, and captioning alongside sign language (Hoskin et al., 2024; Matre & Cameron, 2024; Pavani & Leonetti, 2026; Wood et al., 2018). They report heterogeneous interventions and limited comparative evidence, but they were organized by technology rather than by the condition under which outcomes were measured. A meta-analysis of TTS and read-aloud tools, for example, estimated an average comprehension benefit during supported reading (Wood et al., 2018), which does not address whether reading skill changes when support is withdrawn.

This review therefore asked: among school-age learners with disabilities, what learner-level outcomes are associated with STT, TTS, automatic captioning, adaptive Braille tutoring, and OBR, compared with usual instruction, an alternative accommodation, or a measured baseline? Its specific objectives were to (1) classify each outcome as assisted, unaided, or proximal; (2) synthesize findings within these categories by technology function; (3) appraise design and reporting limitations, including overlapping cohorts; and (4) derive implications for evaluating technology with individual learners. The contribution lies in this outcome framework and in a source-level re-examination of the evidence, rather than in being the first synthesis of these technologies.

# 2. Materials and Methods

## 2.1. Protocol, Registration, and Reporting

The review was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420261513927) and is reported according to PRISMA 2020, the PRISMA-S extension for searches, and the Synthesis Without Meta-analysis (SWiM) guideline (Campbell et al., 2020; Page et al., 2021; Rethlefsen et al., 2021); completed checklists are provided as supplementary files. Several methods were changed after registration, some after data extraction had begun: operational clarification of eligibility, a supplementary search, the outcome classification, explicit linkage of related reports, and replacement of formal risk-of-bias ratings by structured critical appraisal. Each change and its reason are listed in Table S4. Because these decisions were made post hoc, the synthesis should not be read as fully prespecified. The review used published aggregate data only.

## 2.2. Eligibility Criteria

Participants attended primary or secondary education and had a disability affecting access to spoken or written classroom language, including sensory impairment, specific learning disability, intellectual disability, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), or another explicitly identified condition. Eligibility required a diagnosis, an individualized education program (IEP) or equivalent designation, or a standardized assessment with a stated selection threshold; descriptions such as “struggling” or “at risk” alone were insufficient. Where ages were reported, the mean or median had to be 5–18 years and no participant older than 19 years; younger school-enrolled children could be included within an otherwise eligible sample. Explicit primary or secondary school enrolment was accepted when ages were incompletely reported. Mixed samples were eligible only when findings for eligible learners could be extracted separately.

Eligible interventions were automatic STT for writing or dictation, automatic speech recognition producing captions, synthesized TTS, adaptive Braille tutoring, and learner-facing OBR. Multicomponent packages were eligible when a target technology was central and the comparison evaluated that package or technology configuration. Comparators could be usual instruction, another accommodation, a different technology setting, or a measured baseline. Prerecorded human read-aloud, pronunciation verification without dictation, and handwriting recognition were outside scope, as were comparisons that held the technology constant and varied only other instruction.

At least one learner-level outcome was required: reading or listening comprehension, reading rate or accuracy, written productivity or quality, spelling, curricular attainment, Braille acquisition, participation, engagement, independence, or task burden. Technical metrics alone were insufficient. Eligible designs were randomized and nonrandomized group comparisons, within-participant comparisons, single-case designs with repeated observations across phases, and comparative observational analyses of use versus nonuse, which were interpreted as associations. Simple A–B single-case designs were retained but interpreted descriptively. Uncontrolled group pre–post studies, descriptive surveys, qualitative-only studies, and uncontrolled usability evaluations were excluded.

Reports had to be full-length, peer-reviewed, English-language journal articles published by 23 July 2026. Theses, conference papers, reviews, and protocols were not included as independent reports, although related theses or precursor reports could be used to check eligibility and cohort linkage; their numerical results were not attributed to a journal article. Journal articles accessible only as abstracts were included provisionally when eligibility was sufficiently supported, with extraction restricted accordingly. Reports lacking decisive eligibility information were classified as awaiting classification.

## 2.3. Information Sources and Search Strategy

The searches were conducted in two phases. In Phase 1, PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus were searched on 22 July 2026 and APA PsycInfo on 23 July 2026, from inception. Strategies combined school-age and disability terms, technology terms, and design terms. Phase 1 was supplemented by screening the first 200 Google Scholar results (23 July 2026), backward and forward citation searching, publisher website searches, and verification searches. In Phase 2, ERIC was searched on 2 October 2026 through its public application programming interface, using technology and disability terms without the design block (267 records), and targeted searches of publishers, repositories, and related reports were used to resolve candidate reports. Phase 2 retained the 23 July 2026 publication cutoff. Full strategies, dates, and yields are given in Table S1.

The PsycInfo host platform, the Web of Science collections, the original database exports, and complete query histories for the Phase 1 supplementary methods were not retained, so Phase 1 cannot be reproduced in every detail. IEEE Xplore, the ACM Digital Library, CINAHL, trial registries, and dissertation databases were not searched, and the strategies were not peer reviewed using PRESS.

## 2.4. Selection Process

In Phase 1, records were deduplicated and screened by title and abstract and then in full text by one reviewer (A.K.D.); decisions were checked by both authors, but screening was not performed independently in duplicate. Full-text exclusion reasons were classified as publication type or design, technical outcomes only, population or setting, intervention, comparator or phase structure, and insufficient information.

In Phase 2, ERIC records were screened against the same criteria with the assistance of a generative artificial intelligence tool (Section 2.9), and decisions were recorded at record level. Duplicates within ERIC and records corresponding to reports already included in Phase 1 were identified separately. Candidate reports were assessed using the most complete primary source accessible; a bibliographic match alone was not sufficient. At the same time, every report included in Phase 1 was re-checked against its original source and the clarified criteria. Each excluded or unresolved candidate is listed with a reason in Table S6.

Reports describing the same participants were linked using authors, sample characteristics, setting, design, and intervention. Linked reports were retained as separate sources of distinct comparisons but were not treated as independent replications (Table S5). A total number of unique participants was not calculated, because several cohorts may overlap and some reports provide student–item observations rather than participant-level denominators.

## 2.5. Data Collection and Source Verification

A.K.D. extracted data using a structured form covering citation, country, design, participants, eligibility evidence, technology and dose, comparator, outcome and measurement condition, time point, numerical findings, variability, attrition, and implementation. In Phase 2, extracted data for all included reports were compared with the most complete accessible primary source, and the depth of verification was recorded as full text or limited (publisher abstract, preview, figure, or linked primary document). Missing standard deviations were not imputed, graphs were not digitized, and effects were not reconstructed from incomplete statistics. Subgroup estimates were distinguished from pooled-sample results. When a source was internally inconsistent, the inconsistency was recorded, tabulated values were preferred to narrative summaries, and statistics that could not be interpreted reliably were omitted. Study authors were not contacted.

## 2.6. Outcome Classification and Synthesis

Outcomes were classified by the condition of measurement. Assisted outcomes were measured while the technology or accommodation was available. Unaided outcomes were measured without it; the passage of time alone did not establish unaided measurement. Proximal outcomes were curriculum-specific or trained-item measures of acquisition, such as mastery of targeted Braille contractions. Outcomes whose measurement condition could not be determined were classified as unclear. Reading efficiency, comprehension, writing accuracy, and participation were treated as distinct constructs.

Reports were grouped by technology function, outcome category, and design, and synthesized narratively (Campbell et al., 2020; McKenzie & Brennan, 2019). Reported means, contrasts, test statistics, measures of precision, and nonoverlap indices were retained where interpretable. Meta-analysis was not undertaken because the reports did not provide sufficiently comparable estimates with measures of precision across contrasts that addressed the same question. No common effect-size conversion, vote counting of favorable studies, sign test, or ranking of technologies was used. A significant result in one subgroup and a nonsignificant result in another was not interpreted as an interaction, and nonsignificance was not interpreted as equivalence. Observational associations, assisted within-person comparisons, and controlled unaided comparisons were interpreted separately.

## 2.7. Critical Appraisal

Appraisal was planned using RoB 2 for randomized comparisons, ROBINS-I for nonrandomized comparisons, and What Works Clearinghouse standards for single-case designs (Sterne et al., 2016, 2019; What Works Clearinghouse, 2022). On re-checking, the documentation supporting several overall judgments was incomplete or did not support the rating assigned, and complete result-level assessments were not available. Formal tool ratings are therefore not reported. Instead, a structured, source-based critical appraisal considered, for each report, allocation or phase control, confounding and co-interventions, missing observations, outcome measurement, reporting consistency, applicability, and the depth of source access (Table S3). Reports available only as abstracts were not assigned a judgment beyond the limitations evident in the accessible content.

## 2.8. Confidence in the Evidence and Reporting Bias

Confidence in each outcome-specific finding was judged qualitatively with reference to methodological limitations, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and possible missing evidence (Guyatt et al., 2008); formal GRADE ratings were not assigned. Publication bias was not assessed quantitatively. The synthesis states where conclusions depend on abstract-level findings, multicomponent interventions, exploratory subgroups, or potentially overlapping cohorts.

## 2.9. Use of Generative Artificial Intelligence

During Phase 2, ChatGPT (OpenAI; model version not recorded; accessed up to 2 October 2026) assisted with the supplementary searching, screening of ERIC records, identification and checking of original sources, eligibility reassessment, data extraction, critical appraisal, synthesis, drafting, and preparation of tables and figures. This assistance was substantive and does not constitute independent human review. The authors reviewed all outputs and take responsibility for every eligibility decision, extracted value, and interpretation.

# 3. Results

## 3.1. Study Selection

Phase 1 identified 4025 database records and 270 records from other methods (Figure 1). After removal of 1586 duplicates, 2709 records were screened and 2573 excluded. Of 136 reports sought, 8 could not be retrieved; 126 were assessed in full text and 2 from abstracts. Of these 128, 101 were excluded (publication type or design, 27; technical outcomes only, 28; population or setting, 19; intervention, 9; comparator or phase structure, 14; insufficient information, 4), and 27 reports were included (17 from databases and 10 from other methods). The citation-level list of these 101 exclusions was not retained (Table S6).

When the Phase 1 reports were re-checked, Bonifacci et al. (2022) was excluded because its experimental audio consisted of human-recorded words presented to simulate synthesized speech, which falls under the prerecorded-speech exclusion. Two further reports with plausible relevance were confirmed as ineligible: one used prerecorded human speech (Schiavo et al., 2021) and one was a conference paper (Stodden et al., 2012).

In Phase 2, of 267 ERIC records, 2 were duplicates within ERIC and 15 corresponded to reports already included in Phase 1; of the remaining 250, 219 were excluded at title and abstract screening and 31 proceeded to report assessment. Together with 6 candidates from targeted and citation searching, 37 unique candidate reports were assessed: 21 were included, 7 were excluded (Argyropoulos et al., 2009; Daley et al., 2020; Fasting & Lyster, 2005; Felix et al., 2017; Haug & Klein, 2018; Kambouri et al., 2023; Sulaimon et al., 2025), and 9 await classification because decisive population, intervention, or outcome information was not accessible (Douglas et al., 2009; Edeh et al., 2025; Higgins & Raskind, 2004; Leong, 1995; Quinlan, 2004; Schmitt et al., 2011; Wei, 2025, 2026; Witmer & Bouck, 2023). A Braille report published after the cutoff was not eligible (Alsalman, 2026). The review therefore includes 47 reports: 26 from Phase 1 and 21 from Phase 2. Because the Phase 1 record-level exports were not retained, the two phases could not be deduplicated against each other at record level and are shown as separate pathways.

## 3.2. Characteristics of Included Reports

Table 1 summarizes the 47 reports, published between 1999 and 2026 (23 since 2020). Thirty-two evaluated TTS (including one reading pen and one TTS/word-prediction writing study), 13 evaluated STT, one evaluated combined TTS/STT training, and one evaluated an adaptive Braille tutor. Designs comprised 23 single-case designs, 12 within-participant group experiments, 4 randomized group comparisons, 5 nonrandomized group comparisons, 1 group comparison with unverified allocation, and 2 observational analyses of national assessment data. Samples ranged from a single learner to approximately 2790 students. Most participants had reading, writing, or specific learning disabilities, including dyslexia; other reports involved intellectual disability, ADHD, visual impairment, autism, cerebral palsy, traumatic brain injury, and other physical disabilities. The primary outcome was assisted in 34 reports, unaided in 6, proximal in 3, and of unclear measurement condition in 4. Extracted data were verified against the full journal article for 24 reports; for 23, verification was limited to an abstract, preview, figure, or linked primary document. Detailed characteristics and findings are given in Table S2.

Several publications are not independent replications. Raskind and Higgins (1999) and Higgins and Raskind (2000) share 39 participants. Keelor et al. (2018, 2023) are linked to the same 29-child experiment, and overlap with Keelor et al. (2020) is unresolved. Wei (2024) and Ogut et al. (2025) analyzed overlapping 2017 National Assessment of Educational Progress (NAEP) cohorts. Possible overlap among four Swedish reports on learners with intellectual disability, and between the two reports by Meyer and Bouck, also remains unresolved (Table S5).

## 3.3. Methodological Limitations Across Reports

The main threats to validity differed by design (Table S3). Small within-participant experiments often lacked precise paired contrasts, and task equivalence, order, and practice could influence performance. Single-case designs sometimes used short baselines or simple A–B sequences without replicated demonstrations of effect. Instruction, practice, teacher support, and software frequently changed together, so these studies evaluated packages rather than isolated technology effects; multicomponent randomized comparisons were similarly indirect for a software-specific question.

In observational analyses, TTS use was self-selected and potentially related to reading difficulty, item difficulty, and prior familiarity, and statistical adjustment cannot remove such confounding. Attrition affected longer-term comparisons, and missing data were outcome-specific; for example, Chen et al. (2026) analyzed comprehension in 59 learners but eye movements in 47. Several sources contained numerical or descriptive inconsistencies, including Nuraini Herawati et al. (2022), whose discussion refers to two participants without separate trajectories or a defined outcome scale, Keelor et al. (2020), and Ebajay and Malabo (2026). These limitations preclude a reliable common effect estimate or a ranking of technologies.

## 3.4. Speech-to-Text and Assisted Writing

Assisted writing comparisons frequently favored STT for the amount written, whereas findings for quality and accuracy were less consistent. MacArthur and Cavalier (2004) found higher essay quality with STT than with handwriting among students with learning disabilities, with the highest quality under human scribing. Among the 16 eligible learners in Kraft (2023), mean residual errors per 100 words were 5 with STT and 13 with keyboarding, mean text lengths were 92.00 and 79.38 words, and comparative-quality scores were 51.94 and 49.75. These are subgroup descriptive values; the pooled inferential statistics in that report also included 12 peers without difficulties.

In the paired post-test comparison of Flütsch Keravec et al. (2026), 33 STT users produced a mean of 74.12 words in five minutes with STT and 33.55 by hand, with correct writing sequences of 80.77% and 46.04%. Single-case studies that combined STT with modelling, checklists, strategy instruction, or revision support reported heterogeneous gains in productivity and other writing measures (Almgren Bäck et al., 2024; Fälth, Björklund, et al., 2025; Sand et al., 2025). Schneider et al. (2013) similarly combined modality comparisons with writing-strategy instruction in four children with Asperger syndrome: STT yielded longer and more complete stories than handwriting or keyboarding, and the combination of STT and strategy instruction produced the most complete stories. In these studies, the contribution of dictation alone cannot be isolated.

Reports involving physical disabilities broadened the outcome pattern. Garrett et al. (2011) reported greater fluency and text length but lower accuracy with STT than with word processing (five adolescents, a count taken from the companion dissertation). Noakes et al. (2019) and Franklin et al. (2026) reported higher counts of total words, correctly spelled words, and correct writing sequences with STT than with handwriting in three learners with traumatic brain injury and three with cerebral palsy, respectively; higher counts of correct units do not establish a higher proportion correct, and the accessible abstracts did not permit phase-level extraction. Cuifolo et al. (2025) found the strongest writing performance when STT was combined with a graphic organizer in one high-school student with traumatic brain injury. Silió and Barbetta (2010), a writing study, described little change with TTS alone and favorable narrative writing with word prediction, alone or combined with TTS, in six students with learning disabilities.

## 3.5. Text-to-Speech and Assisted Reading

Comprehension findings varied with the task, measure, and comparator. In a randomized crossover experiment with 49 children, Grunér et al. (2018) found faster reading with TTS in both grade groups but better comprehension only in grades 3–5; inattention and hyperactivity symptoms were associated with comprehension gains in opposite directions in the two grade groups. Keelor et al. (2023) found better comprehension in both TTS conditions than in silent reading, without additional benefit from highlighting; the linked predictor report does not establish treatment-effect moderators (Keelor et al., 2018). In the smaller experiment of Keelor et al. (2020), mean comprehension was 2.4 of 3 in each TTS condition and 1.6 in silent reading; inconsistent summary statistics and possible cohort overlap constrain inference, and its synthetic audio-only condition was not treated as a technology-absent control.

Gonzalez (2014) found that full-text TTS supported oral retelling more than print or selective vocabulary support in 17 learners, whereas the multiple-choice comparison was inconclusive (p = 0.052). Higgins and Raskind (2005) reported better comprehension with a reading pen than without assistance in 30 learners; only abstract-level results were accessible. In Dolan et al. (2005), computer-based testing with TTS yielded 65.3% correct versus 58.7% on paper, an inconclusive overall contrast (p = 0.12), with a larger difference for passages longer than 100 words; the reported sample of ten conflicts with the recruitment description, so the analyzed denominator is uncertain.

Izzo et al. (2009) reported mean unit-quiz scores of 5.06 with and 4.07 without TTS, and comprehension scores of 6.98 and 4.85, among seven analyzed learners; changing curriculum units and inconsistent item denominators limit attribution. Camardese et al. (2014) compared an e-reader package with print in 60 students with IEP reading goals; TTS could not be separated from font and dictionary features, and no numerical group contrast was recoverable. Chen et al. (2026) reported a group-by-condition interaction for comprehension and improvement in the ADHD subgroup, but detailed time and speed comparisons in the dyslexia subgroup did not support the reduction in reading effort suggested by the abstract. Silvestri et al. (2022) reported a within-profile benefit only among readers whose listening comprehension exceeded their decoding; differences in subgroup significance do not demonstrate differential response, and a published corrigendum altered signs in one table (Corrigendum, 2021).

Single-case findings included favorable assisted comprehension in Young et al. (2019), Sulaimon and Schaefer (2023), and Svensson et al. (2026), alongside variable or unfavorable comparisons in Meyer and Bouck (2014, 2017), Brunow and Cullen (2021), and Fälth, Nilsson, et al. (2025). Brunow and Cullen found higher comprehension with a human reader than with TTS in all four learners. Schmitt et al. (2019) favored continuous TTS for three of four learners, although the abstract was internally inconsistent about the fourth learner’s reading-pen rate. Moorman et al. (2010) reported higher reading rate and comprehension with TTS in two learners, based on abstract-level information. Taken together, these reports do not support a single best reading modality.

## 3.6. Efficiency, Mathematics, and Assessment Access

Reading rate and task time were distinct from comprehension. In addition to the faster reading reported by Grunér et al. (2018), Alqahtani (2023) reported mean sessions of 5.45 minutes with TTS plus question generation versus 12.73 minutes with repeated reading plus question generation, although the two conditions also differed in task demands. Meyer and Bouck (2017) measured task time, but its direction was not reported in the accessible abstract.

Frankel and Brownstein (2016) compared spoken-mathematics settings in 22 students with blindness or low vision. Explicit end-of-root wording produced higher accuracy than extended pauses for two radical expressions (82% and 95% versus 50% and 68%). These comparisons concern access under alternative speech settings, not acquisition of mathematical knowledge.

Wei (2024) and Ogut et al. (2025) analyzed observed TTS use during the 2017 NAEP grade 8 mathematics assessment. In Wei (2024), binary TTS use was not significantly associated with item correctness among students with disabilities, whereas use frequency was positively associated with correctness among those granted extended time; TTS use was associated with longer response times. In Ogut et al. (2025), overall estimates for students with disabilities were inconclusive across adjustment methods; among students not using extended time, positive associations of 2.4–2.7 percentage points reached p < 0.05 in two of three models. These overlapping observational analyses do not establish a causal accommodation effect, and their student–item observations are not participant counts.

## 3.7. Unaided Skills and Proximal Instructional Outcomes

Evidence of learning without the technology was inconsistent. Two linked STT reports described greater improvement in word recognition, spelling, and reading comprehension after writing practice with STT than after general computer instruction, but nonrandom allocation and shared participants limit confirmation (Higgins & Raskind, 2000; Raskind & Higgins, 1999). In contrast, Flütsch Keravec et al. (2026) did not find a group-by-time advantage of STT for unaided writing quality, text length, handwriting fluency, reading, or motivation over 18 weeks, despite better immediate writing with STT. Svensson et al. (2021), involving 149 learners, found no overall advantage of combined TTS/STT training over usual support immediately or at one year; nonrandom assignment and the loss of about 30% of participants by the final assessment limit interpretation. Park et al. (2017) reported favorable unaided vocabulary and comprehension after about ten weeks of TTS use; the final journal text was not accessible, and inclusion is provisional.

A randomized experiment provided an unfavorable finding. Among 65 readers meeting a standardized reading threshold, Staels and Van den Broeck (2015) found lower delayed orthographic-choice performance after reading with TTS than after independent reading (adjusted means 4.69 versus 5.33 of 8; p = 0.0496), without differences in naming or spelling. This task-specific, short-term result does not establish general harm, but it shows why assisted comprehension and learning need to be evaluated separately.

Smaller reports described unaided gains with weaker controls. Young et al. (2019) reported descriptive increases in unaided oral reading fluency in all four learners and higher Lexile scores in two, as uncontrolled pre–post observations. Sand et al. (2025) reported mixed changes in unaided decoding (8→6, 58→53, 23→28, and 18→20 correct words), and Fälth, Nilsson, et al. (2025) and Svensson et al. (2026) reported uncontrolled pre–post gains or limited decoding improvement. None of these observations establishes a durable learning effect.

Proximal outcomes favored multicomponent packages. Ahlgrim-Delzell et al. (2016) randomized 31 students with developmental disabilities to a TTS-supported phonics package or structured sight-word instruction; mean curriculum-based scores rose from 34.29 to 54.12 and from 32.86 to 37.00, respectively. TTS was not isolated, and inconsistent inferential reporting limits effect estimation. Wood et al. (2020) evaluated combined e-text and systematic instruction for science content in three learners with intellectual disability, with abstract-level findings only. Bhola (2022) and Ebajay and Malabo (2026) reported favorable achievement with TTS-supported teaching, but small samples, researcher-developed measures, unclear assistance during testing, and, in the latter, inconsistent standard deviations limit inference. Nuraini Herawati et al. (2022) reported higher performance during intervention and withdrawal phases, but its undefined scale and incomplete individual data prevent a reliable estimate.

The only Braille report evaluated an adaptive tutor for targeted contractions in ten learners (McCarthy et al., 2016). Mean sessions to mastery were 7.00 with tutor support and 9.58 with teacher instruction alone. Different contraction sets were used in the two conditions, and transfer to untrained contractions or to sustained Braille literacy was not assessed.

## 3.8. Participation, Implementation, and Evidence Gaps

Some reports described preferences, social validity, teacher observations, or perceptions of independence and continued use. These observations inform implementation but do not establish comparative effects on validated measures of school participation, burden, harms, abandonment, or cost. In Fälth, Nilsson, et al. (2025), comprehension nonoverlap values of 8–75% were not measures of independence. No eligible learner-level comparison of automatic captioning or OBR was found; given the search limitations and the reports awaiting classification, this is a gap in the located evidence rather than proof that such research does not exist. # 4. Discussion

## 4.1. Principal Findings

The most defensible conclusion from this evidence concerns the distinction between performance while a technology is in use and learning without it. Speech technologies often increased written output and sometimes improved comprehension under particular task conditions, but they did not consistently improve accuracy, quality, or comprehension across learners and comparators. Controlled unaided outcomes were positive, null, and unfavorable, and the Braille evidence concerned a narrow acquisition target. Of the 47 reports, 34 measured primarily assisted outcomes and only 6 measured unaided outcomes in a controlled comparison. The evidence therefore speaks mainly to access, and much less to learning.

This pattern supports explicit outcome definitions rather than a hierarchy of technologies. It also shows that the inclusion of additional reports does not necessarily add independent evidence: linked publications, shared national assessment cohorts, and companion analyses accounted for several apparent replications. Re-checking reports against their original sources was necessary to establish intervention eligibility (for example, distinguishing synthesized from prerecorded speech) and to separate subgroup from pooled-sample results.

## 4.2. Comparison with Previous Reviews and Possible Mechanisms

A meta-analysis of TTS and related read-aloud tools reported a modest average comprehension benefit (d = 0.35, 95% CI 0.14–0.56; Wood et al., 2018). Its definitions, populations, and contrasts differ from those of the present review, and the estimate concerns supported reading; it should not be transferred to the unaided outcomes examined here. Reviews of STT likewise distinguish compensation during writing from instruction that changes skills (Matre & Cameron, 2024), and the limited Braille evidence is consistent with the need for learner-level evaluation identified previously (Hoskin et al., 2024).

Person–technology matching offers a plausible account of the heterogeneity (Lenker & Paquet, 2003; Scherer & Craddock, 2002). TTS may reduce decoding demands without supplying vocabulary or oral-language comprehension, and STT may reduce transcription demands without supplying planning, organization, or revision. Bypassing print may also alter what is practiced, as the orthographic-learning experiment suggests. These are explanatory hypotheses rather than mechanisms demonstrated across the included studies, and neither a diagnosis nor an isolated subgroup result provides a validated basis for selecting a technology.

## 4.3. Implications for Practice

A practical implication is to define an access goal and a learning goal separately. An access evaluation compares comprehension or written production with and without a specific tool on equivalent tasks. A learning evaluation tests the relevant skill without the tool and, where feasible, after a meaningful delay. Brief alternating-treatments comparisons, as used in several included single-case studies, offer a feasible template for such individual evaluation in schools. Better assisted performance can remain valuable even when unaided skill does not change, and the two goals can be pursued in parallel.

Educational teams can combine such comparisons with assessment of the learner’s language, reading, motor, and sensory profile and preferences. Training and revision support should be documented, because these components may determine whether a tool is usable. Teachers, speech-language and rehabilitation professionals, families, and school health services can use repeated individual observations to review benefit and burden (Council on School Health, 2016; World Health Organization & United Nations Children’s Fund, 2022). Provision also requires time, accessible materials, and continued support (Nordström et al., 2019). These are pragmatic interpretations of heterogeneous evidence rather than graded recommendations: the evidence does not show that technology should replace explicit instruction or human support, and it does not establish the cost-effectiveness of any service model.

## 4.4. Implications for Research

Future studies should state whether their objective is accommodation, skill acquisition, or transfer and measure each under an explicit technology condition. Randomized or adequately replicated single-case comparisons should separate the technology from additional instruction when that is the causal question, and should include delayed unaided outcomes. Reports should provide participant-level eligibility, analytic denominators, intervention fidelity, paired estimates or phase data with measures of uncertainty, and explicit statements about companion reports or shared cohorts. Hypotheses about moderators require prespecified interaction tests with adequate precision. Participation, sustained use, burden, and adverse learning outcomes deserve direct measurement; existing assistive-device instruments may inform this work, although their suitability for children in educational settings needs evaluation (Demers et al., 2002; Jutai & Day, 2002). Searches of education and computing sources are needed before captioning and OBR can be regarded as empty evidence areas.

## 4.5. Strengths and Limitations

The review separates technology functions and measurement conditions, reports unfavorable as well as favorable findings, verifies extracted results against original sources and records the depth of that verification, and makes dependence between reports explicit. The ERIC search and the candidate decisions are documented at record or report level.

The review also has important limitations. The Phase 1 searches used a design block that may have reduced sensitivity, specialist education and computing databases were not searched, and adding ERIC in Phase 2 reduced but did not remove this gap. Phase 1 exports and some supplementary search details were not retained. Screening, extraction, and appraisal were performed by one reviewer with author checking, and Phase 2 relied substantively on a generative AI tool rather than independent duplicate human review; errors in eligibility or extraction are therefore more likely than in a review using duplicate methods. The outcome framework and several eligibility clarifications were finalized after data extraction. English-language and journal-publication restrictions may have introduced selection bias. Nine potentially eligible reports await classification, and 23 included reports could be verified only through abstracts or linked primary documents, so their inclusion and findings are provisional. Formal risk-of-bias ratings were not reported, and the structured appraisal does not provide equivalent result-level judgments. Source inconsistencies, uncertain cohort overlap, sparse precision, and unclear assistance during some outcome tests further restrict inference. These limitations could change both the interpretation of effects and the apparent gaps in the literature.

# 5. Conclusions

Speech technologies can support access to reading and writing tasks for some school-age learners with disabilities, but comprehension, accuracy, and quality outcomes vary across learners, tasks, and comparators. The available evidence does not establish consistent transfer to unaided skills, and one controlled experiment suggests that reading with TTS may reduce some forms of orthographic learning. Evidence on adaptive Braille tutoring is limited to proximal acquisition of trained contractions, and no eligible comparative study of captioning or OBR was located. Educational decisions should distinguish access goals from learning goals, take account of each learner’s response and preferences, and review outcomes under clearly stated measurement conditions.
