## 2.1 Research design and methodological orientation

This study used Interpretive Description, a qualitative methodology developed within nursing to generate contextually grounded knowledge for practice (Thorne, 2025). It suited a question about how mobilisation decisions are reasoned about in routine postnatal care because it aims for interpretations that clinicians can recognise and use. It also allows existing disciplinary knowledge to frame the inquiry without determining its findings. In keeping with this interpretive orientation, participants' accounts were treated as situated explanations of how decisions were made and revised, not as objective records of events. The study therefore did not assess the clinical appropriateness of individual encounters.

Relational autonomy (Entwistle et al., 2010; Stoljar and Mackenzie, 2023) and Figure 1 sensitised the inquiry by directing attention to information, the expression of preferences, requests for assistance or reassessment, and constraints on participation. They did not provide codes or expected findings, and the distinctions in Figure 1 were examined against the data during analysis (Section 2.6).

The phenomenon was reported decisions about first assisted mobilisation and subsequent progression during the initial postoperative admission. The activities included were changing position, sitting on the edge of the bed, transferring, standing and walking. Readiness appraisals, decisions and reconsideration were defined as in Section 1. The study is reported according to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ; Tong et al., 2007), and the completed checklist is provided in Supplementary File S2.

## 2.2 Study setting and mobilisation care pathways

The study took place in Al-Jouf Region, northern Saudi Arabia. In Saudi Arabia, public hospitals are being organised into regional health clusters under the Health Holding Company (Health Holding Company, n.d.). Private hospitals operate alongside this system. Nurses, midwives and physiotherapists are registered as distinct professions by the Saudi Commission for Health Specialties (SCFHS).

Hospitals A and B were government hospitals within Al-Jouf Health Cluster. Hospital C was a private regional hospital outside the cluster. All three provided emergency obstetric surgery and had postnatal wards. After surgery, women were cared for in [the post-anaesthesia care unit] and then transferred to the postnatal ward, where first assisted mobilisation and progression took place. At the participating hospitals, postnatal ward care was provided by [registered nurses working in maternity care], referred to here as maternity nurses. Midwives [practised mainly in labour and delivery units]. Midwives were therefore not recruited as a separate group. [If midwives provided postnatal care at any site, say so and explain how they were treated.]

At all three sites, the pathway followed the same sequence:

1. **Assessment.** A maternity nurse assessed the woman before first assisted mobilisation. The assessment covered [vital signs, pain, recovery of sensation and movement after neuraxial anaesthesia, dizziness, bleeding and attachments such as catheters, drains and infusions].
2. **First mobilisation.** The nurse assisted mobilisation, usually progressing in stages.
3. **Adjusting help.** The nurse adjusted later assistance according to the woman's symptoms and support needs.
4. **Reassessment.** The nurse reassessed the woman [each shift and when new symptoms were reported].
5. **Referral.** Physiotherapy was referral-based. Nurses reported additional rehabilitation needs to the obstetric team, which initiated the referral.
6. **Physiotherapy.** Physiotherapists assessed movement, gave practical guidance and returned recommendations to the maternity team through [the medical record and verbal handover].

Hospitals A and B worked within a cluster framework, but each had its own ward-level procedures. Hospital C used its own hospital pathway. The sites differed in [referral criteria, physiotherapy availability outside routine hours and reassessment arrangements]. Supplementary File S3 compares the sites on assistance roles, referral criteria, referral initiators, physiotherapy availability and reassessment.

Pathway descriptions were drawn from [ward policies and protocols supplied by each hospital, and a structured site-description form completed with each ward's nurse manager and the physiotherapy lead before data collection]. Where written procedures and reported practice differed, both were recorded.

### 2.3.3 Sampling and recruitment

Purposive sampling was used to achieve variation within each group (Palinkas et al., 2015). The variation sought was:

- **Women:** hospital, parity, urgency category, whether labour preceded surgery, anaesthesia type, timing of first mobilisation, whether progression was straightforward or included pausing, deferral or physiotherapy referral, and infant location.
- **Nurses:** hospital, experience, shift pattern and first language.
- **Physiotherapists:** hospital, experience and how often women's health formed part of their caseload.

As the analysis developed, recruitment was directed towards episode types that were under-represented.

**Gatekeepers.** At each hospital, the postnatal ward nurse manager and the physiotherapy lead acted as gatekeepers. They circulated study information but did not approach individuals.

**Recruiting women.** The nurse in charge asked potentially eligible women whether they were willing to hear about the study. With permission, [MAA] gave verbal and written information and allowed at least [12] hours before seeking consent.

**Recruiting professionals.** Nurses and physiotherapists responded directly to the researchers after receiving an information sheet.

**Reducing pressure to take part.** The researchers controlled the following:

- They held no clinical, managerial or supervisory role at any participating hospital.
- Professionals were not recruited through requests from their managers.
- The researchers did not inform managers or care teams who had taken part.
- Participants were told, verbally and in writing, that declining would not affect their care or employment.

Because inpatient recruitment and interviews took place on the ward, staff could sometimes have inferred that a woman was taking part. The researchers reduced this visibility where possible, for example by scheduling interviews at quiet times.

### 2.3.4 Independent accounts

Each group was recruited separately. Professionals were not recruited because of their involvement in a particular woman's care, and they described episodes from their own practice. Comparisons across groups therefore show patterns of reasoning. They do not corroborate accounts of the same encounter.

### 2.3.5 Sample size and adequacy

Sample size was planned using information power (Malterud et al., 2016). This approach holds that fewer participants are needed when the aim is focused, the sample is specific, the dialogue is rich and the analysis is detailed. Empirical studies suggesting that 9 to 17 interviews are often sufficient for focused aims in relatively homogeneous groups (Hennink and Kaiser, 2022) were used only as a reference point, not as a stopping rule.

The planned ranges were:

| Group | Planned range | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Women | 16–20 | Women's accounts were expected to vary most in clinical course, symptoms and newborn-care demands, and needed to come from all three hospitals. |
| Maternity nurses | 10–14 | Nurses are a more homogeneous group, and each could describe many episodes. |
| Physiotherapists | 6–8, or all eligible if fewer | The eligible group was small, and each physiotherapist held specialised information. |

The team judged adequacy in analytic meetings and recorded its reasoning. The question was whether the data were rich, specific and varied enough to answer the research question. In particular, the team considered whether the data included:

- detailed episodes across the different activities and recovery stages;
- episodes in which mobilisation was adapted, paused or deferred, as well as straightforward ones;
- each group's perspective on the main decision points, such as first mobilisation, progression, reassessment and referral.

Interpretations did not have to recur at every site. Interpretations supported across hospitals and groups are reported as such. Insights specific to one group or one hospital are reported with their context.

## 2.6 Data analysis

**Approach.** Analysis followed the iterative logic of Interpretive Description (Thorne, 2025) and proceeded alongside data collection, so that early interpretations informed later sampling and refinement of the interview prompts. The aim was to interpret the reasoning and circumstances surrounding decisions, rather than to list barriers or experiences.

**Coding.** Each transcript was read as a whole, together with its field notes. Inductive codes were then developed, kept close to participants' language at first, and grouped only once relationships between them became apparent. Analytic memos recorded the developing ideas and questions.

**Defining an episode.** An episode was one proposed activity at one point in time, followed from proposal to outcome. The outcome could be attempting, assisting, adapting, pausing or deferring the activity. Three rules applied:

- **Separate decisions.** When a continuous sequence, such as sitting, then standing, then walking, involved a separately reported appraisal or decision at each step, each step was treated as a separate episode. If no separate appraisal or decision was reported, the sequence was one episode.
- **Repeated descriptions.** When a participant described the same episode more than once, the descriptions were merged.
- **Professionals' examples.** A professional's general description of usual practice was coded as an account of practice, not as an episode. Only descriptions of specific occasions were entered as episodes.

**The episode matrix.** Each episode was summarised in a matrix with the following columns:

- activity;
- recovery stage;
- the appraisals reported and who made them;
- the information and concerns behind each appraisal;
- the action taken;
- the reasons given;
- any reconsideration and what prompted it.

The matrix separated what was judged from what was done. It also showed where similar appraisals led to different actions. Episodes are nested within participants' accounts, so they are not independent observations. Each matrix row was therefore linked to its participant and read in the context of that whole account. The matrix supported interpretation and was not used for frequency claims. Supplementary File S4 shows an anonymised trace from an account extract to an episode summary to an interpretation.

**Comparisons.** Comparison was made at three levels:

- within each account, across successive activities;
- within each group, for example by hospital or clinical course;
- across groups.

Because accounts were recruited independently, comparisons across groups were read as patterns of reasoning, not as competing versions of shared events. Comparisons by activity, recovery stage and service context were made where the data supported them, for example episodes within and outside physiotherapy hours.

**Testing interpretations.** Interpretations were tested against the whole data set. The team looked for episodes of agreement, routine episodes and accounts that did not fit. For each main interpretation, it considered alternative explanations, such as staffing pressures or family preferences. The team also examined whether the distinctions in Figure 1 held up in participants' accounts.

### Methodological rigour

**Thorne's criteria.** Rigour was guided by the criteria Thorne (2025) sets out for Interpretive Description:

- **Epistemological integrity.** The research question, interview approach, analysis and claims were kept consistent with one another.
- **Representative credibility.** Variation was sought across sites, groups and clinical courses.
- **Analytic logic.** An audit trail documented the reasoning, including dated versions of the interview guides, field notes, memos, the episode matrix, analytic meeting notes and successive versions of the interpretations.
- **Interpretive authority.** Competing explanations were considered and recorded.

**Transferability.** Readers can judge whether the findings transfer to their own settings using the setting description (Section 2.2 and Supplementary File S3) and the aggregated participant characteristics. Quotations were chosen to show both typical and contrasting accounts.

**External review.** [A qualitative researcher who was not part of the study reviewed de-identified material and preliminary interpretations, which led to [effect].] [Delete if this did not take place.]

**Member checking.** Participants were not asked to validate the findings, because the interpretations synthesise many accounts (Thorne, 2025).
