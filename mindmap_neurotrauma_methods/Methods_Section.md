# 2. Methods

## 2.1. Study design and reporting

This study used a concurrent, three-arm, controlled quasi-experimental design with baseline and post-instruction assessments. Simulation-based learning and assessment were built into the whole study. Students completed simulation-based clinical reasoning assessments at baseline and after instruction. Every teaching session in all three arms also included a simulated pediatric head and brain injury scenario followed by structured debriefing. Simulation was therefore a common learning and assessment modality, not a feature that distinguished the arms. The three arms were taught in parallel during the same academic term ([second semester, academic year 2025–2026]). Recruitment and baseline assessment took place [dd Month–dd Month 2026], teaching [dd Month–dd Month 2026] and post-instruction assessment [dd Month–dd Month 2026], all within the approved study period of February to May 2026.

Allocation to instructional strategy was nonrandom. Intact laboratory sections, the timetabled units in which pediatric laboratory teaching is routinely delivered, were allocated to arms; students were not assigned individually. Students are placed in sections by the faculty timetable before the term begins. Moving individual students between sections would have disrupted scheduled teaching and increased the risk of contamination within sections. ## 2.2. Theoretical rationale and assessment framework

Generative Learning provides the conceptual rationale for comparing instructional activities that require learners to select relevant information, organise relationships and integrate new content with prior knowledge (Fiorella and Mayer, 2016). The arms offered these processes in different ways. Students who constructed maps had to select and organise case content themselves. Students who examined, annotated and revised clinically reviewed AI-generated maps worked from a supplied organisation that directed their attention towards interpretation and adaptation. Case analysis without maps also required active interpretation and justification. The Clinical Reasoning Cycle anchored the assessment domains. It links considering the patient situation and collecting and processing cues to identifying problems, establishing goals, taking action, evaluating outcomes and reflecting (Levett-Jones et al., 2010).

Both frameworks were specified during intervention development [and recorded in the study protocol dated dd Month 2025]. Generative Learning informed the design of the arm-specific activities. The Clinical Reasoning Cycle informed four shared elements: the seven case-analysis headings used in all arms (Section 2.8), the facilitator feedback criteria, the clinical review of AI-generated maps and the primary assessment rubric. These links explain the educational comparison. They do not assume that any strategy is superior, and the proposed learning processes were not measured or tested as mediators. Cognitive load, engagement and cognitive offloading were not measured.

Students could not choose or change their section or instructional strategy.

**Facilitators.** Each of the [n] facilitators taught at least one section in each arm. This crossed facilitators with strategies so that instructor effects were not confounded with arm. No arm was represented by a single section, facilitator or teaching day. [Report the actual number of sections and facilitators per arm.]

**Masking.** Students and facilitators could not be masked to the learning activity. Outcome raters were masked to both arm and assessment occasion. All baseline and post-instruction recordings were renamed with random codes, mixed together and rated in random order only after post-instruction data collection had ended. Before each recording, students were reminded not to mention their teaching activity, and raters recorded any slips. After each recording, raters noted their guess of arm and occasion so that the success of masking could be described. The statistician analysed data with arms coded X, Y and Z until the primary model was finalised.

**Standardisation and contamination control.** All arms received the same core content, pre-class materials, case set, scheduled contact time, simulation practice, debriefing method and facilitator feedback criteria (Table 1; Supplementary Table S1). Arm-specific materials and student work were collected at the end of each session and not released until after post-instruction assessment. Students were asked not to share session materials or discuss them with students in other sections until the study ended. At post-instruction assessment, students reported on a brief contamination questionnaire whether they had (a) seen or used materials from another arm, (b) used generative AI to study the neurotrauma content, (c) received additional tutoring, and (d) spent self-reported additional study time on the content.

## 2.7. Educational materials and AI-map development

**Curriculum blueprint and cases.** The research team developed a curriculum blueprint linking the six learning objectives to four session topics, eight fictional teaching cases (an index case and a variant case for each session) and four separate assessment cases (Section 2.9; Supplementary Table S2). Developmental stage, mechanism of injury, severity, setting and key cues varied systematically across the cases. The team comprised [n pediatric nursing faculty, a pediatric critical care nurse specialist and a pediatric neurosurgeon or intensivist]. Case content was checked against the pediatric guidance cited in Section 2.3. All case material was fictional and contained no identifiable patient information.

**Pre-class materials (common to all arms).** For each session, students received through [the faculty learning management system] the following:

- a narrated slide presentation of about [25] minutes
- a [4–6]-page reading guide
- a short non-graded self-check quiz

Pre-class materials were identical across arms, contained no mind maps, and required about [60] minutes per session.

**AI-map generation.** Maps for the AI-map arm were generated by [two] educators using [platform and provider, e.g., ChatGPT, OpenAI; model: name/version, or "underlying model not disclosed by the provider"], accessed through [web interface/subscription tier] between [dd Month and dd Month 2026]. The consumer interface did not expose generation parameters such as temperature, so default settings were used. Each map was generated in a new conversation with chat history, memory and personalisation features switched off, to avoid carry-over between prompts. The input for each map consisted of the fictional index case, the session learning objectives, the seven Clinical Reasoning Cycle headings and a team-written summary of the relevant guideline key points. No student data or copyrighted text was entered. The complete prompt is reported in Supplementary Box S1.

The AI was asked to return a hierarchical outline with no more than three levels and short keyword nodes. Three independent generations were produced for each case. Two educators independently scored each generation with the AI-Map Clinical Review Checklist (Supplementary Table S6), and the highest-scoring generation went forward to clinical review. All raw outputs were archived with their dates. The selected outline was converted into a radial mind-map layout using [free software, e.g., Markmap (open source) or diagrams.net]. Formatting (layout, colours, icons) was logged separately from changes to content, so that AI generation could be distinguished from human editing.

**Clinical review.** Three reviewers independently checked each selected map node by node using the AI-Map Clinical Review Checklist: [a professor of pediatric nursing, a pediatric critical care nurse specialist and a pediatric neurosurgeon or intensivist, each with at least n years' experience]. The checklist covers:

- factual accuracy
- agreement with the cited guidance
- age appropriateness of normal values and interventions
- consistency with the nursing scope of practice
- safety and correct ordering of priorities
- omission of critical cues or actions
- clarity

For each node, reviewers decided to retain, modify, delete or add content. Disagreements were resolved at a consensus meeting. Every change was recorded in an error log by category: factual error, unsafe or mis-ordered priority, unsupported content, omission, outside nursing scope, or not age-appropriate. Final maps were approved, version-numbered and frozen on [date], before teaching began. The proportions of AI-generated nodes that were retained, modified, deleted or added are reported descriptively. The original outputs, the error log and examples of the final maps are provided in Supplementary S1.

## 2.8. Instructional strategies and delivery

**Common structure.** The intervention ran over [five] weeks. In week 0, each arm had a [30]-minute orientation session. Four weekly 180-minute teaching sessions followed (Supplementary Table S1). Total contact time was therefore [750] minutes per arm, plus about [240] minutes of common pre-class preparation.

The orientation session used the same non-neurological practice case ([pediatric dehydration]) in every arm. Each arm was introduced only to the representation it would use: reading, annotating and revising a supplied map; constructing a map; or completing the structured case-analysis worksheet.

All arms used the same seven case-analysis headings derived from the Clinical Reasoning Cycle:

1. the child and situation
2. cues
3. interpretation
4. prioritised nursing problems
5. goals
6. actions and rationale, including escalation
7. evaluation and reassessment

The arms differed in how students worked with these headings: as the main branches of a supplied map, of a self-constructed map, or of a linear worksheet. They also differed in whether the organised content was supplied or constructed by the students. Each 180-minute session followed the same timed sequence in all arms:

- **Readiness check (15 minutes).** A non-graded check of pre-class learning, with clarification of questions.
- **Arm-specific case work on the index case (75 minutes).** Individual work for 30 minutes, small-group work for 30 minutes, then facilitator feedback for 15 minutes.
- **Simulation prebriefing (10 minutes).**
- **Simulated scenario based on the variant case (50 minutes).** Two runs, each a 10-minute scenario followed by a 15-minute debriefing, with two to three active students and the others observing with a structured observer guide.
- **Consolidation (20 minutes).** Students updated their map or worksheet in light of the variant case and the debriefing.
- **Summary (10 minutes).**

Each student held an active role in at least two scenarios over the four sessions. Scenario design, prebriefing and debriefing followed the Healthcare Simulation Standards of Best Practice (Watts et al., 2021; Persico et al., 2025; Decker et al., 2025). Debriefing used the PEARLS framework (Eppich and Cheng, 2015) in all arms.

Facilitators gave feedback in all arms against the same five Shared Facilitator Feedback Criteria:

1. accuracy and completeness of cues
2. interpretation against age-specific norms
3. prioritisation with rationale
4. safety and scope of actions, including escalation
5. the evaluation and reassessment plan

No arm received model answers during the study. Facilitators attended [two] standardisation workshops ([8] hours in total) covering the arm protocols, the feedback criteria and PEARLS debriefing. Facilitators did not rate outcomes.

**AI-map arm.** Students received a printed A3 colour copy of the clinically reviewed AI-generated map for the index case. For 30 minutes they worked individually, examining and annotating the map with a standard key: highlighting the nodes supported by cues in the case, numbering the priorities, writing a rationale beside each action and drawing links between cues and problems. In small groups (30 minutes) they revised the map to fit the specific child, removing irrelevant nodes, adding case-specific nodes, reordering priorities and adding links. They recorded a one-line justification for each change in a revision log. Students then applied the revised map in the simulated variant case and in the consolidation phase. Students did not interact with generative AI at any point. Their direct use of a chatbot was not part of the intervention.

**Student-map arm.** Students constructed their own mind map for the index case on blank A3 paper. The central node and the seven common headings were pre-printed as main branches, and no generative AI was used. They built maps individually for 30 minutes, selecting the relevant case information and adding sub-branches, keywords, colours and cross-links. In small groups (30 minutes) they compared their maps and built a consolidated group map with a revision log. They then applied it in the simulated variant case and the consolidation phase.

**No-map comparator.** Students analysed the same index case using a structured worksheet in which the seven common headings appeared as linear prompts, with no mind maps. They worked individually for 30 minutes and then, in small groups (30 minutes), produced a consolidated worksheet with a justification log. They applied it in the simulated variant case and the consolidation phase. Contact time, practice and feedback matched the other arms.

**Tailoring, fidelity and completion.** Facilitators could vary the pace of discussion within each block but could not change block timing, materials, the feedback criteria or the case content. Completion of teaching was defined as attending the orientation and at least three of the four sessions. The following fidelity indicators were recorded:

- attendance
- completion of pre-class material, from learning-management-system logs
- submission of the arm-specific product (annotated map, constructed map or worksheet), photographed at session end
- delivery of each feedback criterion, from a facilitator log
- adherence to the timed session structure

An observer who did not teach the session completed the fidelity checklist for a random [25%] of sessions in each arm. All changes from the planned delivery and their reasons were documented.

**Equity.** After post-instruction assessment, all participating students received the materials of the other arms: the AI-generated maps, the map-construction guide and the case-analysis worksheets.

## 2.9. Outcomes and assessment procedures

**Primary outcome.** The primary outcome was each student's clinical reasoning performance score. It was measured with the Pediatric Neurotrauma Clinical Reasoning Evaluation Rubric (PN-CRER), an observer-rated rubric developed for this study. The PN-CRER contains eight items, each corresponding to one phase of the Clinical Reasoning Cycle:

1. considering the child's situation
2. collecting cues
3. processing information
4. identifying problems
5. establishing goals
6. taking action
7. evaluating outcomes
8. reflecting

Its structure follows the approach of the Clinical Reasoning Evaluation Simulation Tool (CREST), which combines observed responses to deterioration with students' explanations (Liaw et al., 2018; Bayzat and Dinc, 2025). The items, anchors and scoring rules were written for pediatric head and brain injury and no CREST items were reproduced, so the PN-CRER is an adaptation, not a validated version of CREST. Its validity evidence comes from this study (Section 2.10). No validity is claimed from the original instrument.

Each item is scored from 0 to 3 against behaviourally anchored descriptors and a case-specific scoring key that lists the critical elements for that case:

- **0:** not demonstrated, or unsafe.
- **1:** fewer than half of the critical elements, or a major misinterpretation.
- **2:** at least half of the critical elements, with minor omissions and no unsafe element.
- **3:** all critical elements, accurate, justified and timely.

The item scores sum to a case score of 0 to 24, with higher scores indicating better reasoning (Supplementary Table S3). Raters coded each element as either *performed* (observed during the scenario) or *stated* (described only in the explanation). Where an action could have been performed in the scenario but was only stated, item 6 was capped at 2. No separate global rating was added to the item total.

**Assessment cases and parallel forms.** Two parallel forms (A and B) were developed, each containing two previously unseen cases. The forms were matched on developmental stage (one young-child case and one school-age case per form), injury severity, number and type of cues, decision points, critical actions, equipment and time (Supplementary Table S2). None of these cases was used in teaching. Within each section, half the students were assigned to Form A at baseline and Form B after instruction, and the other half to the reverse order. Form order was entered as a covariate.

**Assessment procedure.** Students were assessed one at a time without access to AI, maps, notes or other materials, at baseline and [5–7] days after the final teaching session. The procedure for each case was:

1. A standard 3-minute orientation to the room and equipment, with the same written instructions for every student.
2. A 10-minute scenario on [the pediatric manikin], with a trained standardised parent and a simulated patient monitor. Cues were delivered at fixed times (0, 4 and 8 minutes), so every student met the same deterioration regardless of what they did. Scripted local responses to student actions were allowed; for example, oxygen saturation rose when oxygen was applied. A simulated physician answered escalation calls with a scripted reply.
3. A 5-minute structured reasoning interview.

The interview was conducted by an assessor who had not taught the student. The assessor asked the same five neutral questions in a fixed order, with only the neutral prompt "Anything else?" (Supplementary Box S2). Continuous think-aloud was not used. All scenarios and interviews were video-recorded.

**Secondary outcomes.** *Knowledge* was measured with the Pediatric Neurotrauma Nursing Knowledge Test (PNNKT), a 40-item single-best-answer test developed from the curriculum blueprint and written to established item-writing guidelines (Haladyna et al., 2002) (Supplementary Table S4). About 70% of the items are clinical vignettes at the application or analysis level (Anderson and Krathwohl, 2001). Each item scores 1 for correct and 0 for incorrect or unanswered, giving a range of 0 to 40. The same items were used on both occasions, with item and option order rearranged. Neither answers nor scores were released before the study ended.

*Procedural skills* were measured with the Pediatric Neurotrauma Procedural Skills Checklist (PNPSC). This 15-item observation checklist was completed at a separate 10-minute station (Supplementary Table S5). Tasks were given as explicit written instructions, such as "perform and document a complete neurological observation", so the station measured accuracy of execution rather than clinical decision-making. Each item scores 0 (not performed), 1 (performed incompletely or incorrectly) or 2 (performed correctly), giving a range of 0 to 30.

The three outcome scores were analysed separately and never combined into an overall total.

**Order of assessment.** On each occasion the order was the same: the clinical reasoning simulation first, then the skills station, then the knowledge test. This prevented the test items from cueing answers in the case. No delayed retention assessment was conducted. [If a delayed assessment was actually collected, describe it here.]

## 2.10. Instrument suitability, pilot testing and reliability

**Content validity.** A panel of [seven] experts reviewed the PN-CRER (items, anchors and case keys), the four assessment cases, the PNNKT and the PNPSC: [three pediatric nursing faculty, two pediatric critical care nurse specialists, one pediatric neurosurgeon or intensivist, and one nursing education and measurement specialist]. Experts rated each item for relevance on a 4-point scale and commented on clarity, completeness, suitability for the learner level and coverage of the reasoning domains. They also rated whether each pair of parallel cases was equivalent on the matching criteria.

The item-level content validity index (I-CVI) and the scale-level index using the averaging method (S-CVI/Ave) were calculated. The prespecified criteria were I-CVI ≥ 0.78 and S-CVI/Ave ≥ 0.90 (Lynn, 1986; Polit et al., 2007). Items below the criterion were revised and re-rated, or deleted. [Report the actual values and revisions.]

**Language.** All materials were presented in English, the language of instruction. Students could give their reasoning explanations in English or Arabic, and all raters were bilingual. Reasoning was scored on content, not on fluency, and the scoring manual told raters to disregard grammar and vocabulary. [If Arabic versions were produced: translation, independent back-translation, reconciliation by an expert committee and cognitive testing with ten students followed established guidance (Beaton et al., 2000).]

**Pilot study.** The pilot was conducted [two weeks] before baseline with [20] students who were not eligible for the main study ([e.g., fourth-year students who had completed the pediatric course]). It checked scenario timing, the visibility and audibility of cues, the clarity of the anchors and case keys, recording quality, assessment burden and floor or ceiling effects. It also provided item analysis for the PNNKT. Items were revised if their difficulty fell outside 0.30–0.80 or their corrected point-biserial discrimination was below 0.20. [Report the problems identified and the changes made.] Pilot participants did not contribute to the main sample, and no main-study student had been exposed to the assessment cases. Pilot recordings were used only to calibrate raters.

**Rater training and inter-rater reliability.** Two raters scored all PN-CRER recordings independently: [pediatric nursing faculty holding a doctorate who did not teach or allocate study students]. Training took [six] hours and covered the rubric, the case keys, the performed-versus-stated coding rule and [ten] pilot calibration recordings. Each rater had to reach an absolute-agreement intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of at least 0.75 on the calibration set before rating any study recordings (Koo and Li, 2016). Raters scored independently without discussion, and all individual ratings were retained. The primary score was the mean of the two raters, fixed in advance without consensus adjustment.

Inter-rater reliability for the PN-CRER total is reported as a two-way random-effects, absolute-agreement ICC for the mean of two raters, ICC(2,2), because the primary score is that mean. The single-rater ICC(2,1) and 95% confidence intervals are also reported (Koo and Li, 2016). For the PNPSC, ICC(2,1) is reported from the double-rated [25%] sample, because single ratings were used for the remaining recordings.

**Internal consistency.** Reliability is reported separately for each instrument, with a method suited to each. For the PNNKT, Kuder–Richardson 20 was calculated at each occasion, along with item difficulty and discrimination (Kuder and Richardson, 1937); 0.70 or higher was considered adequate for group comparisons (Downing, 2004). For the PN-CRER, Cronbach's α across the eight items is reported as supplementary information only. It does not replace inter-rater reliability. No single pooled reliability coefficient was calculated across instruments, and no published coefficients were presented as local results.

Scores were analysed as continuous variables. No poor/moderate/good performance categories or competence cut-offs were applied, because no external standard-setting procedure had been carried out.

## 2.12. Statistical analysis

**Estimand.** The primary estimand was the difference in mean post-instruction PN-CRER score between the AI-map and student-map arms, adjusted for baseline PN-CRER score and prespecified covariates. It was estimated for all consenting allocated students, analysed in their allocated arm regardless of attendance. Within-arm changes from baseline are reported descriptively only. Separate paired tests within each arm cannot establish a difference between groups.

**Model.** A baseline-adjusted linear mixed model (analysis of covariance) was fitted to the post-instruction score (Vickers and Altman, 2001). The fixed effects were arm, baseline score, assessment form order and the prespecified covariates: previous-year grade point average and number of previous simulation sessions. These covariates were chosen in advance on educational grounds, not on the basis of baseline p values. A random intercept for laboratory section accounted for clustering arising from allocation. Teaching small groups nested within sections were added as a further random intercept where that variance could be estimated. Because the number of sections was small, inference used Kenward–Roger degrees of freedom (Kenward and Roger, 1997). As a sensitivity analysis, the same contrasts were estimated with cluster-robust CR2 standard errors (Pustejovsky and Tipton, 2018).

A baseline-adjusted gain-score model gives the same group contrast. Unadjusted change scores are presented descriptively and were not used for inference (Tennant et al., 2022). Post-intervention variables such as attendance and engagement were not included in the primary model.

**Model checking.** Score distributions, floor and ceiling effects, residual normality and variance across arms, the linearity of the baseline–outcome relationship and the arm × baseline interaction were examined. If assumptions were not met, the prespecified alternatives were robust standard errors or a transformation. They were applied and reported as sensitivity analyses, without switching models according to significance.

**Reporting of estimates.** For each arm, adjusted means (estimated marginal means) with 95% confidence intervals are reported. The primary result is the adjusted AI-map minus student-map difference in original score units, with its 95% confidence interval and two-sided p value at α = .05. A standardised effect size is also given: the adjusted difference divided by the pooled baseline standard deviation of the outcome.

**Supporting and secondary analyses.** The supporting comparisons (AI-map versus no-map and student-map versus no-map) were analysed with the same model. Holm's procedure was applied within this family of two (Holm, 1979). Knowledge and procedural skills were analysed with the same model structure, each adjusted for its own baseline score. Holm's procedure was applied within the family of six secondary contrasts (two outcomes × three pairwise contrasts). Estimates and confidence intervals are reported for all contrasts, whatever their significance.

**Baseline comparability.** Baseline comparability was described with means, standard deviations or frequencies by arm and with standardised mean differences. Absolute standardised differences above 0.10 were flagged descriptively (Austin, 2009).

**Missing data.** Missingness is reported by arm and reason. Under a missing-at-random assumption, missing outcomes were handled by multiple imputation by chained equations: [m ≥ 20] imputations, with an imputation model containing arm, all baseline scores, the analysis covariates, section indicators, and attendance as an auxiliary variable (Sterne et al., 2009; White et al., 2011). Last observation carried forward was not used. Three sensitivity analyses were run:

- a complete-case analysis
- a delta-adjusted analysis that shifted imputed values by 0.2 and 0.5 standard deviations in each arm in turn, to test robustness to data missing not at random
- a per-protocol analysis restricted to students who completed teaching (Section 2.8), excluding those who reported contamination

**Masking assessment.** Raters' guesses of arm and occasion are compared descriptively with chance agreement.

**Software and interpretation.** Analyses were performed in R [version 4.x.x] (R Core Team, [2025]) using the lme4, lmerTest/pbkrtest, emmeans, clubSandwich, mice and irr packages. The statistical analysis plan was finalised on [date], [before / after] post-instruction data were available. Any analytical decision made after data inspection is identified as such. A nonsignificant difference is not interpreted as equivalence. Because allocation was nonrandom, adjusted differences are interpreted as comparisons of instructional strategies, not as an isolated causal effect of AI generation.
