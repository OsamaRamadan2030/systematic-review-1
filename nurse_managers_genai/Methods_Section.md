# When Is Intervention Warranted? Nurse Managers' Judgements About Informal Generative Artificial Intelligence Use in Clinical Nursing in Saudi Arabia — An Interpretive Description

## 1. Introduction

Generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) creates text and other content in response to prompts, with applications in healthcare documentation, patient information and professional learning. Its accessibility allows clinicians to experiment with tools beyond formally implemented systems. However, generated content can be inaccurate, incomplete or biased, and the information entered can create privacy and security risks [1]. For nursing management, these concerns become immediate when staff incorporate generated material into clinical work, and managers must determine what oversight is warranted.

In this study, informal GenAI use was defined as nurse-initiated use outside an established, explicitly governed clinical workflow. The definition encompassed uses subject to unclear local permissions and those that contravened explicit restrictions. Related literature uses the term "shadow artificial intelligence" for unauthorised applications, including tools accessed outside organisational procurement and oversight [2]. Quantitative context is available from a 2026 online survey in the United States and United Kingdom: 87 of 281 nurses (31.0%) reported accessing artificial intelligence for work through personal, non-employer-provided tools [3]. This figure describes personal access to artificial intelligence more broadly; it does not establish the prevalence of unauthorised GenAI use.

Nurse managers connect organisational expectations with the supervision of clinical nursing and the implementation of new technologies [4]. Informal use requires judgements about the nursing task, the information entered, potential consequences and the adequacy of verification. Responses may include guidance, additional checking, consultation, restriction or escalation. Decisions to withhold or defer intervention also warrant examination. Understanding these decisions requires attention to managers' knowledge, workload, access to expertise and authority to act, alongside how they explain expectations and allocate responsibility.

Existing research has already addressed informal use and managerial involvement. Zhang and colleagues' multi-stakeholder interpretive-description study examined nursing interns' clinical placement experiences and included head nurses and nursing administrators, identifying discretionary rules, risk appraisal and uneven verification [5]. Jia and colleagues' grounded-theory study in the *Journal of Nursing Management* examined value co-creation between nurses and GenAI, including verification, accountability and organisational safeguards [6]. Building on these studies, the present enquiry focused on nurse managers' intervention judgements concerning registered nursing staff, and examined how managers justified action or non-action across episodes with clear restrictions and uncertain permissions. Its analytical focus was the reasoning within supervisory decisions.

Saudi Arabia was a relevant setting because healthcare digital transformation is accompanied by national ethical expectations and data-protection duties. The Health Sector Transformation Program, within Vision 2030, promotes digital transformation to improve healthcare access and quality [7]. The Saudi Data and Artificial Intelligence Authority's AI Ethics Principles address privacy, safety, transparency, human oversight and accountability [8]. The Personal Data Protection Law classifies health data as sensitive and regulates its access and processing [9]. Its Implementing Regulation requires healthcare data controllers to incorporate applicable requirements into internal policies, allocate responsibilities, document processing and minimise health-data processing [10]. Uncertainty about a tool's local approval therefore coexists with established obligations.

Saudi nursing research has identified organisational support, training, ethical concerns and professional autonomy as important considerations in artificial intelligence adoption [11]. Research with hospital nurse leaders in Hail has also examined perceived professional impact and preparedness for integration [12]. These studies established contextual concerns but provided less detail about how managers interpret informal use, distinguish acceptable experimentation from uses requiring intervention, and communicate their decisions. Before this study, it remained unresolved how national and institutional expectations were translated into responses to specific clinical situations.

An episode-focused enquiry was chosen because it could examine how managers recognised the need for action, reconciled competing considerations and revised responses as circumstances became clearer. Comparing ambiguous cases with uses subject to explicit restrictions could clarify how policy specificity entered managerial judgement. Interpretive description was selected because it supports the development of practice-relevant nursing knowledge while retaining contextual variation [13]. The intended contribution was an account of supervisory reasoning that could inform proportionate oversight, verification responsibilities, staff support and workable escalation arrangements.

## 2. Aim

The aim of this study was to explore and interpret how nurse managers in Saudi Arabia judged whether, when and how to intervene in informal GenAI use in clinical nursing, including situations in which institutional permission for specific tools or tasks was unclear.

### Research Questions

1. How did nurse managers recognise and interpret episodes of informal GenAI use and decide whether, when and how to intervene?
2. How did clinical risks, available safeguards, managerial authority and the clarity of institutional permissions shape these judgements?
3. How did managers justify, communicate and reconsider their responses, and what support did they identify as necessary for effective oversight?

### Objectives

The objectives were to:

- describe the informal GenAI uses encountered by nurse managers and how these uses came to their attention;
- interpret the reasoning underlying decisions to intervene, withhold intervention or seek further information;
- examine variation across episodes with unclear permissions and explicit restrictions, including how managers negotiated authority, verification and accountability; and
- identify implications for nursing management, staff communication and institutional governance grounded in managers' accounts.

## 3. Theoretical Framework

Organisational sensemaking provided a sensitising perspective for examining nurse managers' accounts of informal GenAI use. It was used to draw attention to how organisational actors developed understandings of events and related those understandings to action [14, 15]. These ideas offered questions for enquiry rather than assumptions about the processes managers necessarily followed.

The related concept of sensegiving, which concerns attempts to shape how others understand a situation [16], was considered where participants described efforts to influence others' interpretations of informal use or managerial responses. Its relevance was examined within the accounts, and communication was not automatically classified as sensegiving. The enquiry remained open to responses that did not involve deliberate efforts to influence understanding.

Within interpretive description, theory was used to support critical interpretation while remaining responsive to the practice question [17]. Each interview first invited participants to describe actual situations in their own terms. Follow-up questions within the interview then sought clarification and explored the reasoning expressed, with theory-informed probes used flexibly. Analysis attended to contextual variation, divergent cases and explanations extending beyond the sensitising concepts. These concepts did not supply fixed coding categories. Reflexive analytic memos documented how theoretical ideas influenced interpretation and how they were retained, modified or set aside in response to the data [18]. ## 4. Methods

### 4.1 Design and Rationale

This study used a qualitative interpretive description design [13, 19], selected for three reasons. First, the research questions concerned an applied problem, and the aim was to produce knowledge that could inform management practice and governance rather than formal theory. Second, the design treated contextual variation as analytically important, which suited the study's interest in how hospital context, managerial level and the clarity of permissions shaped judgement. Third, it accommodated the reflexive use of sensitising theory described in Section 3 [17].

Other qualitative designs were considered and judged less suitable. Grounded theory was not selected because its aim of building substantive theory did not match the study's applied interpretive aim. Interpretive phenomenology was not selected because its focus on the essence of lived experience differed from the study's focus on the reasoning within supervisory decisions. A descriptive qualitative design would have limited the interpretive depth needed to explain why managers acted, delayed or decided not to act.

The study took a constructivist, naturalistic position. Managers' accounts were treated as situated interpretations of events, not as direct records of what staff did. Data were generated through episode-focused, semi-structured interviews informed by the critical incident technique [20]. Each manager was asked to describe specific, real episodes of informal GenAI use they had encountered, and the reasoning that led to their response. Anchoring interviews in concrete episodes, rather than in general opinions about artificial intelligence, was intended to reduce socially desirable answers and to make the logic of decision-making accessible for analysis. The **episode** served as the main unit of comparison in analysis, and the **participant** served as the unit of sampling.

### 4.2 Setting and Context

The study was conducted in four public hospitals in the Jouf region of northern Saudi Arabia. Sites were selected purposively to capture differences in hospital size, level of care, clinical specialities and patient acuity. Bed capacity ranged from 200 to 420, and the hospitals provided secondary or tertiary care, including inpatient and emergency services. All four hospitals held accreditation from the Saudi Central Board for Accreditation of Healthcare Institutions (CBAHI) and operated under a shared regional healthcare directorate. This common governance structure provided a comparable administrative context, while differences in service provision supported examination of contextual variation across sites.

To protect institutional anonymity, the hospitals were referred to as Sites A to D. Each site used an electronic health record and had institutional policies on information security, acceptable use of information technology and clinical documentation. Before data collection, the research team reviewed these policies, with permission from each hospital's nursing directorate and information technology department, to describe the governance context in which managers worked. At the time of the study, none of the four hospitals had approved a GenAI tool for clinical nursing use, and none had a policy that named which GenAI tools or tasks were permitted. However, all four sites had existing rules that prohibited entering patient-identifiable information into external, non-approved platforms, in line with national data-protection requirements [9, 10]. This mix of explicit restrictions on some actions and unclear permission for others matched the contrast the study was designed to examine. The policy review was used only to understand the context and to inform interview probes; it was not analysed as primary data.

The study was conducted between April 2026 and September 2026. Interviews took place between April 2026 and August 2026, and analysis continued alongside and after data collection until September 2026.

### 4.3 Participants, Sample and Sampling

#### Eligibility

In this study, a *nurse manager* was defined as a registered nurse who held formal line-management responsibility for registered nursing staff working in direct clinical care. This included first-line managers (head nurses and unit managers) and middle managers (nursing supervisors and assistant directors of nursing with responsibility for clinical areas).

Inclusion criteria were:

1. a current nurse manager role, as defined above, in one of the four participating hospitals;
2. at least 12 months' experience in a nursing management role, so that participants could draw on a range of supervisory situations;
3. direct supervisory responsibility for registered nurses in inpatient, critical care, emergency or outpatient clinical areas;
4. experience of at least one episode, within the previous 12 months, in which they had become aware of, been told about or had reason to suspect informal GenAI use (as defined in Section 1) by registered nursing staff in clinical nursing work; and
5. ability to take part in an interview in Arabic or English.

Exclusion criteria were: (a) a role with no line-management responsibility for clinical nursing staff (for example, roles limited to education, quality or infection control); (b) current membership of the research team or a current academic or employment relationship with any researcher (for example, enrolment in a course taught by a team member); and (c) extended leave during the data-collection period.

Criterion 4 was required because the study focused on episodes. Managers who had never encountered or suspected informal use could not have provided the episode-level data that the research questions required. The 12-month window was chosen to support detailed recall while capturing the recent spread of GenAI tools.

#### Sampling strategy

Purposive, maximum-variation sampling [21] was used to include managers whose judgements were likely to differ in meaningful ways. A sampling matrix was set up before recruitment and monitored throughout. It covered:

- **hospital site** (Sites A–D; at least five participants per site);
- **managerial level** (first-line and middle management);
- **clinical area** (medical, surgical, critical care, emergency, maternity and paediatrics, outpatient and other specialities);
- **years of management experience** (fewer than 5 years, 5–10 years, more than 10 years);
- **gender** and **nationality** (Saudi and non-Saudi nurse managers), reflecting the composition of the regional nursing workforce; and
- **type of episode encountered** (use involving an explicit restriction, use where permission was unclear, or both).

As analysis progressed, sampling also became partly theoretical in the sense used in interpretive description [13]. Later recruitment gave priority to managers who were likely to add contrasting or under-represented episodes, such as managers in high-acuity areas, managers who had escalated an episode and managers who had chosen not to intervene.

#### Sample size

Sample size was planned using the principle of information power [22] and checked against empirical evidence on sample adequacy in interview studies [23–25]. Under the information power model, the more relevant information a sample held, the fewer participants were needed. Information power was judged across five dimensions: study aim, sample specificity, use of established theory, quality of dialogue and analysis strategy. The assessment of each dimension was summarised in Table 1.

**Table 1. Assessment of information power used to plan the sample size**

| Dimension [22] | Study characteristic | Effect on required sample |
|---|---|---|
| Aim | Moderately broad: three research questions covering recognition, judgement, communication and support needs | Increased the number needed |
| Sample specificity | Dense: all participants held managerial responsibility and had direct experience of relevant episodes | Decreased the number needed |
| Established theory | Sensitising concepts (organisational sensemaking and sensegiving) used to guide enquiry, not to test hypotheses | Moderate effect |
| Quality of dialogue | Episode-anchored interviews by experienced interviewers, in the participant's preferred language | Decreased the number needed |
| Analysis strategy | Cross-case, cross-episode comparison across four sites and two types of permission clarity | Increased the number needed |

Taken together, these dimensions indicated a moderate sample. Empirical evidence provided a further reference point. A systematic review of empirical tests had found that saturation in interview studies with relatively homogeneous samples was usually reached within 9 to 17 interviews [23]. Code saturation had been reported at about 9 interviews, whereas meaning saturation, the full understanding of issues, had required 16 to 24 interviews [24]. Because this study needed comparison across four sites, two management levels and two categories of permission clarity, a target range of **20 to 28 participants** was set in advance. This range allowed at least five participants per site and gave enough episodes in both the explicit-restriction and unclear-permission categories to support comparison.

The decision to stop recruitment did not rely on numbers alone. It was based on the analytic sufficiency of the data for answering the research questions, which fitted the interpretive aims of the study [13, 26]. Recruitment ended when all three of the following conditions were met:

1. the sampling matrix was filled, with at least five participants from each site and representation across management levels and clinical areas;
2. each of the two permission categories (explicit restriction and unclear permission) included at least ten detailed episodes with enough information about context, reasoning and response for comparison; and
3. in the three most recent interviews, no new type of episode or dimension of reasoning appeared that changed the developing interpretive account. This was assessed with a stopping-criterion approach adapted from Guest and colleagues [25] and recorded in analytic memos.

These conditions were met after 25 interviews. The final sample therefore included **25 nurse managers**, which fell within the planned range and was consistent with the evidence above.

### 4.4 Recruitment and Consent

After ethical and institutional approval was obtained, the nursing directorate at each hospital gave the research coordinator a list of nurse managers who met the role criteria (criteria 1 to 3). Hospital administrators played no further role in recruitment and were not told who was invited, who agreed or who declined, to prevent pressure to take part and to protect participants from identification within their organisation.

The research coordinator was a trained research assistant who was not a member of the research team and had no employment or line-management relationship with any eligible manager. The coordinator sent invitations by institutional email. Each invitation included a bilingual (Arabic and English) participant information sheet. The information sheet explained the study aim, what participation involved, how confidentiality would be protected, how data would be handled, the limits of confidentiality (Section 4.10) and the right to withdraw. Interested managers contacted the coordinator directly. The coordinator then confirmed eligibility, including criterion 4, in a short screening conversation. To build the planned variation in the sample, invitations were sent in waves guided by the sampling matrix.

Of 38 managers identified as meeting the role criteria, 31 were invited across the recruitment waves. Of these, 25 agreed to take part, 4 declined because of workload and 2 did not respond after one reminder. The other 7 eligible managers were not invited because their profile was already well represented in the sampling matrix when recruitment ended. No participant withdrew after the interview.

Participants had at least 72 hours to read the information sheet and ask questions before giving written consent (Section 4.10). For interviews conducted by videoconference, the signed consent form was returned electronically before the interview. At the start of each interview, the interviewer confirmed consent verbally, including consent to audio recording. No financial or other incentive was offered.

### 4.5 Data Collection

#### Interview guide

Data were collected through individual, semi-structured, episode-focused interviews. The interview guide (Supplementary File 1) was developed from the research questions, the literature reviewed in the Introduction, the national governance context [7–10] and the critical incident technique [20]. It had five parts:

1. **Opening and role context:** the participant's role, unit and how GenAI was talked about in their workplace.
2. **Episode elicitation:** a request to describe, in their own words, one to three specific episodes of informal GenAI use they had encountered. For each episode, this covered how it came to their attention, the nursing task involved, the information entered and what they understood about local permissions.
3. **Judgement and response:** what the manager considered, what they did or chose not to do, when they did it, who they consulted and why.
4. **Communication and reconsideration:** how they explained their response to staff and others, how others reacted and whether they later changed their view or response.
5. **Support and governance:** the knowledge, authority, expertise and institutional arrangements they considered necessary for effective oversight.

Opening questions in each part were open and non-leading. Theory-informed probes (for example, about cues that something needed attention, or about efforts to shape how staff understood an episode) followed only after the participant's own account, as described in Section 3. The guide was prepared in English, translated into Arabic by a bilingual team member and checked by a second bilingual team member. Differences were resolved through discussion to ensure conceptual equivalence rather than word-for-word correspondence [27].

#### Pilot interviews

The guide was piloted with two nurse managers who met the role criteria and worked at a public hospital outside the study sites. One pilot interview was conducted in Arabic and one in English. Following the pilot, the order of parts 2 and 3 was changed so that judgement questions followed directly from each episode, and a prompt was added to ask about episodes in which the manager decided not to intervene. Pilot data were not included in the analysis.

#### Interview procedures

Before the interview, participants were asked to think of specific episodes to discuss. They were reminded in writing not to share the names of staff or patients or any other identifying details. A short demographic and professional questionnaire recorded age group, gender, nationality, managerial level, clinical area, years of nursing and management experience, highest qualification and any training received on artificial intelligence.

Interviews were conducted by two members of the research team (one female and one male) with doctoral-level training in qualitative methods. Participants could choose the interviewer's gender, the interview language and the interview format. Interviewers had no prior relationship with any participant; the information sheet introduced them as nurse researchers at Jouf University and explained their reasons for conducting the study. No one other than the participant and interviewer was present during interviews, and no repeat interviews were conducted. Twenty interviews took place face to face in a private room away from the participant's clinical unit and outside handover times. Five took place by secure, institution-licensed videoconference (Microsoft Teams), with the participant in a private space [28]. Seventeen interviews were conducted in Arabic and eight in English. Interviews lasted between 46 and 84 minutes (median 62 minutes) and were audio-recorded with consent.

Interviewers wrote field notes immediately after each interview. These recorded non-verbal features, the interview atmosphere, initial analytic impressions and reflexive observations. Data collection and analysis ran in parallel, so the guide was refined between interviews to follow up new lines of enquiry. For example, from the ninth interview onward, a probe was added about the difference between "checking the output" and "checking what was entered". All changes to the guide were dated and recorded in the audit trail. At the end of each interview, participants were asked whether they would agree to review a short summary of preliminary interpretations later in the study (Section 4.8).

### 4.6 Data Management

Data were managed in line with the Personal Data Protection Law and its Implementing Regulation [9, 10] and Jouf University data-governance policy.

- **Recording and storage:** Interviews were recorded on encrypted digital recorders. Within 24 hours, files were transferred to password-protected, encrypted storage on the Jouf University secure server and then deleted from the recorder. Signed consent forms and the linkage key were stored separately from interview data, and only the principal investigator could access the linkage key.
- **Pseudonymisation:** Each participant received an identification code made up of a site code and a participant number (for example, B-NM07). During transcription, all names, unit identifiers, staff and patient details and any other potentially identifying information were removed or replaced with generic descriptors (for example, "[staff nurse]" or "[surgical unit]").
- **Transcription:** Recordings were transcribed verbatim in the language of the interview by two trained research assistants who had signed confidentiality agreements. The interviewer checked each transcript against the audio recording for accuracy and completeness.
- **Translation:** Arabic transcripts were analysed in Arabic so that meaning stayed close to the participants' own words, and codes were written in English. Excerpts chosen for reporting were translated into English by a bilingual team member and checked against the Arabic original by a second bilingual team member, with the aim of preserving meaning, idiom and tone rather than literal wording [27]. Disagreements were resolved by discussion and recorded.
- **Exclusion of GenAI from data handling:** Given the study topic and the confidentiality of the data, no GenAI or cloud-based automated tool was used for transcription, translation, coding or interpretation. Only the research team had access to the data.
- **Software:** NVivo 14 (Lumivero) was used to organise data, codes, memos and analytic matrices. The project file was stored on the secure university server.
- **Retention:** Audio recordings were kept on the secure server until analysis was completed in September 2026 and were then destroyed. De-identified transcripts and analytic files were placed under a five-year post-publication retention schedule, in line with institutional policy, with secure destruction scheduled at its end.

### 4.7 Data Analysis

Analysis followed the interpretive description approach [13, 19]. It began with the first interview and was iterative throughout. Analysis moved from close reading of individual accounts, to comparison across episodes and participants, to a coherent interpretive account that answered the research questions. It involved five overlapping phases, which were summarised in Table 2.

**Table 2. Analytic phases**

| Phase | Activities | Outputs |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Immersion | Repeated listening to recordings and reading of transcripts and field notes; initial reflexive memos on first impressions and on the possible influence of the researchers' assumptions | Interview summaries; reflexive memos |
| 2. Broad-based inductive coding | Line-by-line coding of transcripts close to participants' language, without imposing sensitising concepts; two researchers independently coded the first five transcripts and met to compare and discuss interpretations | Shared, evolving coding framework with definitions |
| 3. Episode-level analysis | Each episode was extracted and described in an episode matrix covering: how it came to the manager's attention, the nursing task, information entered, permission status (explicit restriction or unclear permission), perceived risk, available safeguards, response (for example, guidance, additional checking, consultation, restriction, escalation, seeking further information, deferral or non-intervention), justification, communication and later reconsideration | Episode matrix in NVivo [29] |
| 4. Constant comparison | Comparison within and across participants, episodes, sites, managerial levels and permission categories; focused search for divergent and negative cases; examination of when and how sensitising concepts helped explain the data, and where they did not | Comparative memos; refined patterns |
| 5. Interpretive synthesis | Development of an integrated account of how managers judged whether, when and how to intervene; testing of the account against the full data set; linking of the account to implications for practice and governance | Final interpretive account; selection of illustrative excerpts |

Sensemaking and sensegiving were introduced only in phase 4, as sensitising concepts [30] used in the manner set out in Section 3. Accounts that did not fit these concepts, such as responses involving no deliberate attempt to shape others' understanding, were retained and analysed in their own right. The episode matrix allowed systematic comparison of explicit-restriction and unclear-permission episodes, directly addressing research question 2 and the third objective. Divergent cases (for example, managers who tolerated use that others would have stopped) were used to refine the interpretation rather than set aside.

The full team met every two weeks during analysis to discuss codes, matrices and developing interpretations. Consistent with the interpretive paradigm, the purpose of having two coders was to deepen interpretation through discussion rather than to reach statistical agreement; inter-rater reliability coefficients were therefore not calculated [26, 31]. Disagreements were resolved by returning to the data and were recorded in memos.

### 4.8 Rigour and Trustworthiness

Trustworthiness was addressed using Lincoln and Guba's criteria [32], alongside Thorne's quality criteria for interpretive description: epistemological integrity, representative credibility, analytic logic and interpretive authority [13]. Epistemological integrity was addressed through alignment of question, design and analysis (Sections 2–4.7); representative credibility through variation across sites and roles; analytic logic through the audit trail; and interpretive authority through reflexivity and member reflections. The strategies for each Lincoln and Guba criterion are described below.

#### Credibility

Credibility was supported by prolonged engagement with the data across the six-month study period and by interview procedures that encouraged detailed, candid accounts (Section 4.5). Investigator triangulation was achieved through independent coding of initial transcripts and regular team discussion. Data were triangulated across four sites, two management levels and a range of clinical areas. The policy review (Section 4.2) provided contextual triangulation for how participants described the permission environment.

Peer debriefing took place with an experienced qualitative nurse researcher outside the research team, who reviewed the coding framework, episode matrix and draft interpretation at two points.

Member reflections [33, 34] were used in place of transcript return. A two-page summary of preliminary interpretations, in Arabic and English, was sent to the 21 participants who had agreed to review it, and 14 responded. Their comments were treated as further data that could deepen or challenge the interpretation, not as a test of whether it was "correct". For example, several participants pointed out the role of informal consultation with peers before formal escalation, and this was developed further in the final account.

#### Dependability

A detailed audit trail was kept. It included dated versions of the interview guide, the coding framework and its revisions, the episode matrix, analytic and reflexive memos, minutes of team meetings and records of analytic decisions. Data collection procedures were standardised through an interviewer briefing and a joint review of the first two interviews by both interviewers. The audit trail was reviewed by a team member who had not conducted interviews, to check that analytic decisions were traceable and consistent.

#### Confirmability

Confirmability was supported by reflexivity (Section 4.9) and by grounding each interpretive claim in excerpts from several participants, reported with participant codes so that readers could see the range of sources behind each claim.

#### Transferability

Transferability was supported by thick description [32] of the national governance context, the hospitals, the policy environment and the characteristics of participants and episodes, allowing readers to judge the relevance of the findings to their own settings. Maximum-variation sampling was intended to show the range of managerial reasoning rather than a single typical pattern. The final account was presented together with the conditions under which its patterns appeared (for example, managerial level or permission clarity), so that readers could consider how far it applied to other healthcare systems with similar digital transformation and data-protection requirements.

### 4.9 Reflexivity and Positionality

The research team consisted of four nurse researchers based at Jouf University. All held doctoral degrees and had experience in qualitative research. Two had previously worked as nurse managers in Saudi public hospitals, one had expertise in nursing informatics and digital health, and one in nursing ethics and governance. Two team members were Saudi and two were non-Saudi Arabic speakers, and the team included both women and men. No team member worked at any participating hospital or had an employment or supervisory relationship with any participant.

The team recognised that its position could shape the research in several ways [35]. Team members with nurse management experience brought insider understanding of supervisory pressures, which helped build rapport and design relevant probes. However, this experience could also have led them to assume shared meanings or to identify with managers' justifications. The team member with informatics expertise brought knowledge of GenAI capabilities and risks, which could have led to judgements about participants' technical understanding. All team members had used GenAI tools in academic work. They held neither strongly supportive nor strongly opposing views about its use in clinical nursing, but they acknowledged that their views on appropriate use could influence how they interpreted managers' decisions.

As university academics, the researchers may have been seen by participants as linked to formal authority or to an evaluation of their decisions, which could have encouraged participants to present their responses favourably. To reduce this risk, interviewers explained at the start of every interview that the study did not judge whether managers' decisions were right or wrong. They also stressed that no information would be shared with the hospitals, and they avoided signalling approval or disapproval of reported actions.

Reflexivity was practised throughout the study. Each team member kept a reflexive journal, and interviewers wrote reflexive field notes after each interview. During team meetings, the team discussed how members' backgrounds might be influencing interpretation. Members with management experience were invited to question interpretations that closely matched their own past practice, and members without management experience were invited to question interpretations that appeared to judge managers too quickly. These discussions were recorded in the audit trail.

### 4.10 Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Jouf University Institutional Review Board (Protocol No. 7603) and from the ethics committees of all participating hospitals prior to recruitment. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection. Participation was voluntary, and participants were informed of their right to withdraw at any stage without occupational consequences.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki [36] and the Saudi Law of Ethics of Research on Living Creatures and its Implementing Regulations [37]. Data already provided could be withdrawn, without giving a reason, until analysis was completed on 30 September 2026. The study raised four particular ethical issues, which were addressed as follows.

**Protection from occupational risk.** Participants described their own supervisory decisions, some of which could have been seen as departing from policy. In addition to the recruitment, interview and pseudonymisation safeguards described in Sections 4.4–4.6, findings were reported so that no individual, unit or site could be identified. Where a combination of characteristics could have identified a participant (for example, a rare clinical area at a particular site), these details were reported in general terms.

**Third parties.** Participants described the conduct of staff who had not consented to the study. Beyond the safeguards in Sections 4.5 and 4.6, episodes were reported only at a level of detail that did not allow individuals to be identified.

**Limits of confidentiality and disclosure.** The information sheet and consent form explained that confidentiality would be kept unless a participant disclosed an ongoing situation posing a serious risk of harm to patients that had not already been addressed through institutional procedures. A disclosure protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board. Under this protocol, the interviewer would pause the interview, discuss the concern with the participant and encourage them to report it through the appropriate institutional channels; the researchers would not report it themselves unless required by law. No disclosure requiring this protocol occurred during the study.

**Participant well-being.** Discussing difficult supervisory decisions could have caused discomfort. Interviewers were prepared to pause or stop interviews, and participants could skip any question. Interviews were scheduled at times convenient to participants and did not interfere with clinical duties.
