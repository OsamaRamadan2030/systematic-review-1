**Study:** Whose Readiness Counts? Women's, Maternity Nurses' and Physiotherapists' Accounts of Mobilisation Decisions After Emergency Caesarean Birth

## Part B. Women's interview guide

Conducted in Arabic, in a private room, at the bedside when neighbouring beds were unoccupied, or by telephone or video after discharge.

### B0. Before the interview

- Confirm with the nurse in charge that the woman is alert, comfortable and has not recently been sedated (inpatient interviews).
- Confirm the woman's willingness to start now, and offer to rearrange the time around feeding, rest or visits [PPI].
- Remind her that she can pause, move elsewhere, skip or defer any topic, and stop at any time.
- If a companion is present, confirm the companion's separate written consent for incidental recording. Explain that the interview is about the woman's own experience and that the companion's words will not be used.
- Remote interviews: confirm written consent was received beforehand, confirm consent verbally at the start of recording, and check privacy and comfort.

### B1. Opening [P]

1. Could you tell me about the first time you got out of bed after your baby was born?
   - Prompts: when it was, who was with you, what you were asked to do, how it went.

### B2. Background to the birth (brief)

2. Could you tell me a little about how your baby came to be born by caesarean?
   - Prompts: whether you had been in labour beforehand, how quickly things happened, what you knew about recovery before the operation.
   - Use only as much detail as the woman wishes to give. Do not probe the emergency itself beyond what she offers.

### B3. Decision episodes (repeat for each episode the woman describes)

3. Think about a time someone suggested you sit up, stand or walk. What was suggested?
4. How did your body feel at that moment?
   - Prompts: pain, dizziness or feeling faint, bleeding, how your legs felt (after the back injection), sickness, tiredness after labour, the wound, the drip or catheter.
5. What made you feel you could, or could not, do it at that moment?
   - Prompts: what you felt, what the nurse checked or told you, what others in the room said.
   - Do not suggest fear as an explanation. If the woman uses a word like "scared", ask what, specifically, she was worried about.
6. What did you say to the nurse, and what did she say to you?
   - Prompts: whether you were asked how you felt, whether you could say everything you wanted to say, language, whether anyone spoke for you.
7. What happened then?
   - Prompts: tried, did part of it, stopped, waited until later; who decided; why.
8. When was the pain medicine given in relation to getting up? [P]
9. How did looking after your baby fit in with getting up and moving? [PPI]
   - Prompts: feeding, lifting the baby, the baby being in the neonatal unit, timing around feeds.

### B4. Progression and reconsideration

10. How did getting up change over the following days?
    - Prompts: walking to the bathroom, the corridor, the neonatal unit; what made the next attempt possible; whether anything had to be repeated or started again.
11. If you stopped or waited at any point, what happened afterwards?
    - Prompts: whether anyone explained why, whether a next step was agreed, how you felt about it.
12. Was there a time you felt ready and the nurse thought you should wait, or the other way round? [A]
    - Prompts: what each of you thought, how it was settled, how you felt about the outcome.
13. Did getting up feel different at night, or when the ward was busy? [A]

### B5. Other support

14. Did anyone else help or advise you about moving — a physiotherapist, a doctor, family?
    - Prompts: what they showed or told you, whether it helped, when it happened.

### B6. Closing

15. Looking back, what would have helped you most when getting up for the first time?
16. Is there anything else you would like to tell us that we have not asked about?

- Thank the participant and check how she is feeling. Follow the distress protocol if needed (Section 2.8).

## Part C. Maternity nurses' interview guide

Conducted in Arabic or English by the physiotherapist researcher, in a private room away from clinical areas or by video.

### C0. Introduction

- Confirm consent and that participation will not affect employment. Remind the participant not to name women or colleagues.
- Contextual information form completed before the interview (Part F).

### C1. Usual practice (contextual account; not counted as an episode)

1. Could you describe how you usually support a woman to get up for the first time after an emergency caesarean on your ward?
   - Prompts: what you check, the order of activities, written procedures, how this differs from elective caesarean.

### C2. Most recent detailed episode (within the preceding six months)

2. Please think of the most recent time you assessed or assisted a woman's mobilisation after an emergency caesarean that you remember in detail. What activity was proposed, and at what point in her recovery?
3. What did you assess, and what did the woman tell you?
   - Prompts: vital signs, pain, dizziness, bleeding, attachments, sensory and motor recovery after neuraxial anaesthesia, tiredness, newborn care.
4. How did you weigh what you observed against what she told you?
   - Prompts: which information mattered most and why; whether anything was "not negotiable".
5. How did you communicate with her about it?
   - Prompts: questions you asked, explanations given, language, relatives' involvement.
6. What did you decide to do, and why?
   - Prompts: attempt, assist, adapt, pause, defer; the help needed (second staff member, chair, relative).
7. When was analgesia given in relation to the attempt? [P]
8. What happened afterwards, and was the plan reconsidered?
   - Prompts: what you recorded, what you handed over, who reassessed.

### C3. A difficult episode (within the preceding six months)

9. Could you describe a time when a mobilisation decision felt difficult?
   - Use the same prompts as C2.

### C4. Differing appraisals and circumstances [A]

10. Have there been times when your judgement about readiness differed from the woman's, a relative's, a doctor's or a physiotherapist's?
    - Prompts: how it was discussed, whose view shaped what happened, how you felt about it.
11. How are mobilisation decisions different at night or out of hours?
    - Prompts: staffing, availability of help, how you plan attempts.

### C5. Physiotherapy coordination

12. When do you think a woman needs more than routine nursing support to move?
13. What happens when you think she does?
    - Prompts: telling the obstetric team, referral, timing, informal advice from physiotherapists, what happens when physiotherapy is not available.

### C6. Closing

14. What would help nurses make these decisions with women?
15. Is there anything else you would like to add?

## Part D. Physiotherapists' interview guide

Conducted in Arabic or English by the nurse researcher, in a private room away from clinical areas or by video.

### D0. Introduction

- Confirm consent and that participation will not affect employment. Remind the participant not to name women or colleagues.
- Contextual information form completed before the interview (Part F).

### D1. Role and referral (contextual account; not counted as an episode)

1. Could you describe how you become involved with women after emergency caesarean birth at this hospital?
   - Prompts: referral route, information received, timing, your usual assessment.

### D2. Most recent detailed episode (within the preceding twelve months)

2. Please think of the most recent woman you assessed or treated after an emergency caesarean whom you remember in detail. Why were you involved, and what did you know before seeing her?
3. What did you assess, and what did she tell you about moving?
   - Prompts: transfers, sit-to-stand, walking, pain, symptoms, confidence, newborn-care tasks, earlier attempts.
4. What was your appraisal of her readiness, and for which activities?
5. What did you recommend or do, and why?
6. When was analgesia given in relation to your session? [P]
7. How did you communicate your recommendations, and what happened afterwards?
   - Prompts: clinical record, verbal handover to the nurse in charge, follow-up, whether the advice was used.

### D3. A difficult episode (within the preceding twelve months)

8. Could you describe a time when your involvement in a mobilisation decision felt difficult?
   - Use the same prompts as D2.

### D4. Differing appraisals and circumstances [A]

9. Have you found that your appraisal differed from the woman's, the nurse's or the referral information?
   - Prompts: how it was discussed, whose view shaped what happened.
10. What happens for women when physiotherapy is not available, for example at night or at weekends?

### D5. Advice outside formal referral

11. Do nurses or women ask for your advice outside formal referrals? Could you give an example?

### D6. Closing

12. What would make physiotherapy input most useful for women after emergency caesarean birth?
13. Is there anything else you would like to add?
