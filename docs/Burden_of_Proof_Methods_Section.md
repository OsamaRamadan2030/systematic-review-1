# 4. Methods

## 4.1 Design and Rationale

The study used a qualitative interpretive description design to develop a practice-relevant understanding of unit-level nurse managers' reported judgements and responses to nurses' care concerns in Saudi hospitals (Thorne, 2025). Interpretive description was chosen because the study addressed an applied question arising from nursing management practice; its purpose was to produce knowledge that managers, quality leaders and educators could use, rather than to generate formal theory or describe the essence of a lived experience (Thorne et al., 1997; Thorne, 2025). The design rests on a constructivist, naturalistic orientation. Managers' accounts were treated as situated professional reasoning, not as objective records of what happened in each case.

Participants were nurses with formal managerial responsibility for a clinical unit. The conceptual orientation outlined in the Introduction (soft intelligence, problem-sensing versus comfort-seeking, and burden of proof) acted as sensitising concepts (Blumer, 1954). These concepts shaped interview probes about evidence, responsibility and follow-up but were not used as a predetermined coding framework. Analysis proceeded inductively through comparisons within and across managers' accounts, so interpretations could challenge or extend the sensitising concepts. The inquiry remained open to concerns that managers accepted without further substantiation as well as those that prompted further investigation.

## 4.2 Setting and Context

The study was conducted in six public hospitals in the Jouf region of northern Saudi Arabia. Sites were selected purposively to capture differences in hospital size, level of care, clinical specialities and patient acuity. Bed capacity ranged from 200 to 420, and the hospitals provided secondary or tertiary care, including inpatient and emergency services. All six hospitals held accreditation from the Saudi Central Board for Accreditation of Healthcare Institutions (CBAHI) and operated under a shared regional healthcare directorate. This common governance structure provided a comparable administrative context, while differences in service provision supported examination of contextual variation across sites.

Clinical units in all six hospitals reported a common set of quality and patient-safety indicators to the hospital quality department each month. These included nursing-sensitive indicators (falls, hospital-acquired pressure injuries, device-associated infections and medication-administration incidents), incident-reporting counts and patient-experience scores. Results were displayed on unit and hospital dashboards, compared with organisational targets and national benchmarks, and reviewed at monthly quality meetings, consistent with national guidance (Saudi Patient Safety Center, 2025). Unit-level nurse managers (locally titled *head nurse* or *nurse manager*) were accountable for unit indicator performance and reported to the hospital Director of Nursing. Nursing workforces included Saudi and internationally educated nurses, and both Arabic and English were used for professional communication.

## 4.3 Participants, Sample and Sampling

**Eligibility.** Eligible participants were registered nurses who held a substantive head nurse or nurse manager post for a clinical inpatient, critical care or emergency unit that reported routine quality indicators. They also had at least six months in their current role, so they could describe at least one full cycle of indicator review, and could be interviewed in Arabic or English. Acting managers with less than six months in post and anyone with a supervisory, employment or academic relationship with the research team were excluded. Full criteria are provided in Supplementary File 3.

**Sampling strategy.** Purposive maximum-variation sampling was used to capture a broad range of managerial reasoning across organisational contexts (Thorne, 2025). Before recruitment, the team defined four sampling dimensions: (a) hospital (six sites); (b) unit type (medical, surgical, critical care, emergency, paediatric and maternity); (c) years of managerial experience (<5, 5–10 and >10 years); and (d) country of nursing education (Saudi Arabia or abroad). Coverage of each dimension was monitored throughout data collection. As analysis progressed, sampling became analytically informed. Among the managers who had volunteered, the team prioritised those whose accounts were likely to deepen developing comparisons, such as managers of units with consistently on-target dashboards and managers who had recently escalated a concern.

**Sample size.** Sample size was planned using the concept of information power (Malterud et al., 2016). Information power was judged to be relatively high for four reasons: (a) the aim was narrow and focused on a specific decision problem; (b) participants were highly specific to that aim, since all held the role and experience under study; (c) established sensitising concepts informed the inquiry; and (d) interviews anchored on concrete recent incidents were expected to produce focused, in-depth dialogue. However, comparing reasoning across six hospitals and six unit types called for more participants than a single-site study would require. Empirical studies of interview samples were used only as a rough benchmark (Hennink et al., 2017; Hennink & Kaiser, 2022). On this basis, the team set a provisional range of 18 to 25 participants, with at least three per hospital and at least two per unit type.

Recruitment ended when two conditions were met. First, each hospital and each unit type was represented (not every combination of the two). Second, the research team judged that the interpretive account had reached sufficiency for the study aims (Thorne, 2025). The account was judged sufficient when it offered a coherent, well-illustrated interpretation for each of the four objectives, included contrasting and negative cases, accounted for variation across hospitals and unit types, and was unlikely to change in its practical implications with further interviews. This judgement was recorded in the audit trail and was reached after 23 interviews. The final sample comprised **23 unit-level nurse managers** across six hospitals (three to five per hospital) from medical (*n* = 5), surgical (*n* = 5), critical care (*n* = 5), emergency (*n* = 3), maternity (*n* = 3) and paediatric (*n* = 2) units. Participant characteristics are reported in Results (Table 1).

## 4.4 Recruitment and Consent

After ethical approval, written permission was obtained in January 2026 from the regional healthcare directorate and from each hospital's medical and nursing directors. At each site, the Director of Nursing acted only as a gatekeeper. The Director sent one standard invitation, with the participant information sheet and the researchers' contact details, to all eligible managers (*N* = 38) by official hospital email. Managers who wished to take part contacted the research team directly. From that point, the research team, not the Directors, managed all communication, sampling decisions and scheduling.

Twenty-nine managers volunteered. The research team invited volunteers to interview in waves, according to the sampling dimensions and developing analysis. Of the 29 volunteers, 23 were interviewed. Six were not: two could not be scheduled because of annual leave, one withdrew before interview citing workload, and three volunteered after interpretive sufficiency had been reached. These three were thanked and told that recruitment had closed. No participant withdrew after interview.

To protect participants' anonymity from gatekeepers, the research team arranged interviews directly with each participant. Interviews were held at times the participant chose, in rooms in non-clinical education or training areas away from nursing administration. The research team booked these rooms under a generic study title, without participant names. Participants could also choose an off-site or video interview. Directors of Nursing were not told who volunteered, who was interviewed or when.

Participants received the information sheet in Arabic and English and had at least 48 hours to consider taking part. Written informed consent was obtained before each interview, and consent to audio recording was reconfirmed at the start of the recording. Participants could withdraw their data without giving a reason until 14 days after their interview. No incentives were offered.

## 4.5 Data Collection

Data were collected between January and June 2026. Each participant took part in one semi-structured, in-depth interview and completed a brief demographic and professional-profile questionnaire.

**Interview guide.** The interview guide (Supplementary File 1) was developed from the study objectives, the literature reviewed in the Introduction and the sensitising concepts. It covered:

1. Unit context and the quality indicators the manager used.
2. A critical-incident prompt (Flanagan, 1954) asking the manager to describe a recent occasion, within the past 12 months, when a nurse raised a care concern while unit indicators appeared reassuring.
3. Probes on how the concern was interpreted, what made it credible, whether further information was sought, what kind of information and from whom, and what investigation, action, escalation, follow-up and feedback followed.
4. A contrasting incident, such as a concern acted on without further substantiation or one that was not pursued.
5. Organisational conditions that enabled or constrained the manager's response.

Anchoring the interview in specific incidents was intended to reduce generalised or socially desirable accounts.

Two senior nurse leaders and one qualitative methodologist reviewed the guide for relevance, clarity and cultural appropriateness. It was translated into Arabic and back-translated by two bilingual nurse researchers, with discrepancies resolved to preserve conceptual equivalence (Squires, 2009). In early January 2026, after ethical approval and before data collection began, the guide was piloted in two interviews with nurse managers from a Jouf hospital that was not a study site. The pilots led to minor changes in wording and question order, and pilot data were excluded from analysis. During data collection, probes were refined in response to developing analysis. For example, probes were added on informal channels, such as messaging applications, through which nurses raised concerns. These refinements stayed within the scope of the approved guide, and every change was logged in the audit trail.

**Interview procedures.** All interviews were conducted by the first author (R1), who was trained in qualitative interviewing and had no supervisory, employment or evaluative relationship with any participant. Participants chose the interview language and format:

- **Language:** Arabic, *n* = 14; English, *n* = 9.
- **Format:** face to face, *n* = 18; video, *n* = 5. Video interviews used an institutionally licensed Microsoft Teams account, with only the participant and interviewer present.

Interviews lasted 42 to 81 minutes (mean 58 minutes) and were audio-recorded. Participants were asked not to name patients or colleagues. R1 wrote field notes immediately after each interview, covering the setting, rapport, emerging ideas and reflexive observations.

## 4.6 Data Management

Data were managed in accordance with the Saudi Personal Data Protection Law (Kingdom of Saudi Arabia, 2021) and institutional policy, and all data were stored within Saudi Arabia. No automated transcription or generative-AI tools were used in data handling or analysis.

A professional bilingual transcriptionist based in Saudi Arabia (domestic transcription), bound by a confidentiality agreement, transcribed the recordings verbatim in the language of each interview. R1 checked every transcript against its audio and de-identified it, using codes P01–P23 for participants and H1–H6 for hospitals.

Two research support roles had confidential access to de-identified transcripts only: a bilingual nurse researcher who checked quotation translations, and an independent qualitative methodologist who served as peer debriefer and auditor. Audio recordings were deleted after verification. De-identified data will be kept for five years after publication. Transcripts will not be shared publicly because the participant population is small and identifiable. Detailed storage, access and retention procedures are provided in Supplementary File 3.

## 4.7 Data Analysis

Analysis followed the iterative, inductive logic of interpretive description (Thorne, 2025) and ran concurrently with data collection from February to August 2026, so that early interpretations informed later sampling and probing. NVivo 14 (Lumivero) was used to organise data, codes, memos and matrices.

Both analysts, R1 and R2, are bilingual in Arabic and English. They analysed Arabic transcripts in Arabic and English transcripts in English, using English code labels, so that no meaning was lost through translating whole transcripts. Only quotations selected for reporting were translated into English. R1 translated them, and the bilingual support researcher checked them for conceptual equivalence (Squires, 2009).

Analysis proceeded in six overlapping steps:

1. **Immersion.** R1 and R2 listened to the recordings and read each transcript and its field notes several times, writing initial analytic memos.
2. **Broad initial coding.** R1 and R2 independently coded the first four transcripts (two in Arabic and two in English), using open descriptive and interpretive codes. Comparing and discussing their codes produced a provisional coding framework. Because the aim was interpretive depth rather than agreement on fixed categories, inter-coder reliability statistics were not calculated. Differences in interpretation were treated as analytic material and resolved through discussion.
3. **Constant comparison.** R1 coded all remaining transcripts. R2 independently coded a further eight, chosen to span all six hospitals, both languages and contrasting incident outcomes, and reviewed R1's coding of the rest. Matrices compared reasoning by hospital, unit type, managerial experience, type of concern (for example staffing, missed care, patient deterioration, equipment or professional behaviour) and outcome (accepted, investigated, escalated or not pursued). The coding framework was revised as understanding developed, and all versions were kept.
4. **Engagement with sensitising concepts.** After inductive coding of the first eight interviews, developing codes were compared deliberately with the sensitising concepts to identify where managers' accounts supported, complicated or contradicted them. Data that did not fit were retained and explored.
5. **Negative and contrasting case analysis.** Two kinds of account were examined systematically to test and refine developing interpretations: concerns that managers accepted without further substantiation, and cases where indicators later proved to have missed a problem.
6. **Interpretive synthesis.** Codes were grouped into broader patterns and then into an interpretive account, guided by the question "What does this mean for nursing management practice?" (Thorne, 2025).

Interpretations were discussed at approximately fortnightly team meetings (14 meetings in total), attended by R1, R2 and R3, and with the peer debriefer. The final output was a set of interpretive themes and a practice-oriented framework for assessing concerns alongside indicators.

## 4.8 Rigour

Rigour was established using Thorne's (2025) criteria for interpretive description. Table 2 shows how the study's strategies address each criterion.

**Table 2. Strategies addressing Thorne's criteria for rigour in interpretive description**

| Criterion | Meaning | Strategies used in this study |
|---|---|---|
| **Epistemological integrity** | The research question, design and analytic procedures are consistent with the study's philosophical and disciplinary stance. | Applied question derived from nursing management practice. Constructivist orientation, with accounts treated as situated reasoning. Sensitising concepts used as lenses, not as a coding framework. Interpretive sufficiency used as the stopping point. No inter-coder reliability statistics, in keeping with interpretive aims. |
| **Representative credibility** | Claims are proportionate to the range and depth of data from which they are drawn. | Maximum-variation and analytically informed purposive sampling across six hospitals and six unit types. Interviews anchored on concrete incidents. Negative and contrasting case analysis. Optional member reflection: all 23 participants were offered a two-page bilingual summary of preliminary interpretations, and 10 responded. Their comments were treated as additional data rather than validation and refined one theme on informal follow-up (Smith & McGannon, 2018). Quotations drawn from across participants and sites. |
| **Analytic logic** | The reasoning from data to interpretation is explicit and traceable. | Audit trail recording sampling decisions, guide refinements, dated versions of the coding framework, memos and meeting notes. Independent coding by two bilingual analysts. Cross-case matrices. The independent methodologist audited the trail after interview 12 and at the end of analysis. |
| **Interpretive authority** | Interpretations go beyond the researchers' own assumptions and can be trusted to represent shared patterns. | Reflexive journal (Section 4.9). Analysts from different professional backgrounds challenged one another's interpretations. Peer debriefing. Each interpretation was checked against coded data from multiple participants. |

To help readers judge whether the findings apply to their own settings, the paper describes the governance and indicator context (Section 4.2), participant characteristics (Table 1) and the conditions under which concerns were raised.

## 4.9 Reflexivity and Positionality

The research team comprised three members, all bilingual in Arabic and English:

- **R1:** a nurse academic with doctoral training in qualitative methods, who previously held a unit-level nurse manager post in a Saudi hospital outside the study region.
- **R2:** a nursing researcher experienced in qualitative health services research, with no clinical or managerial role in Saudi hospitals.
- **R3:** a nurse leader with experience in hospital quality and patient safety.

No team member had employment, supervisory or evaluative relationships with participants or study sites.

R1's insider position as a former nurse manager eased rapport and helped in understanding indicator-reporting language. It also risked assuming shared meanings and sympathising with managerial constraints. R1 managed this risk by using clarifying probes (for example, "What do you mean by 'proof' here?"), taking a non-evaluative stance and keeping a reflexive journal after each interview and analytic session (Olmos-Vega et al., 2023). In analytic meetings, R2's outsider perspective and R3's quality-governance perspective were used deliberately to challenge interpretations that appeared either to excuse or to criticise managers.

Hierarchical and accountability pressures could have led managers to present their responses favourably. The team addressed this in four ways: emphasising confidentiality, making clear that the study did not evaluate individual performance, asking for both "successful" and "difficult" incidents, and attending to hesitations and contradictions in accounts.

## 4.10 Ethical Considerations

The Institutional Review Board of King Abdulaziz Specialist Hospital, Aljouf Health Cluster (committee registration H-13-S-161), approved the study (No. 9804, 26 December 2025). The approval covered one interview per participant, domestic transcription, optional member reflection, and confidential access to de-identified transcripts by the research support roles specified above. Administrative permission was obtained from the regional healthcare directorate and each participating hospital.

The study complied with:

- the Declaration of Helsinki (World Medical Association, 2025);
- the Saudi Law of Ethics of Research on Living Creatures (National Committee of Bioethics, 2010); and
- the Personal Data Protection Law (Kingdom of Saudi Arabia, 2021).

The main foreseeable risks were professional and reputational, because the participant population was small and identifiable. These risks were reduced in several ways. Employers were not told who took part (Section 4.4). Sites and participants are reported only in aggregate, and quotations are attributed only by participant and hospital code. Unit-identifying details were removed. The consent information explained the limits of confidentiality: if a participant disclosed an ongoing, unaddressed risk of serious harm to patients, the interviewer would encourage the participant to use organisational reporting channels. No such disclosure occurred. Detailed risk-mitigation procedures are provided in Supplementary File 3.

## 4.11 Reporting Guideline

The study is reported in accordance with the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR; O'Brien et al., 2014). The completed SRQR checklist and the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist (Tong et al., 2007) are provided as Supplementary File 2.

# Supplementary File 3. Eligibility, Data Management and Ethical Safeguards

**S3.1 Eligibility criteria**

Inclusion criteria:

- Registered nurse.
- Substantive head nurse or nurse manager post with 24-hour accountability for a clinical inpatient, critical care or emergency unit.
- At least six months in the current managerial role.
- Unit reports routine quality indicators to the hospital quality department.
- Able to be interviewed in Arabic or English.

Exclusion criteria:

- Acting or temporary manager in post for less than six months.
- Manager of a non-clinical or outpatient-only area.
- Current supervisory, employment or academic relationship with a member of the research team.

**S3.2 Data management procedures**

- **Storage:** Audio files were transferred on the day of each interview to an encrypted, password-protected, institutionally managed drive accessible only to the research team, then deleted from the recorders.
- **Consent forms and identification key:** Paper consent forms were kept in a locked cabinet. The electronic participant identification key was kept in a separate encrypted file accessible only to the principal investigator.
- **Transcription:** The transcriptionist signed a confidentiality agreement and worked on an encrypted institutional device. R1 checked each transcript against its audio.
- **De-identification:** Names, units, hospitals, dates and potentially identifying events were replaced with codes or generic descriptors.
- **Support roles:** The two support roles accessed de-identified transcripts only, under signed confidentiality agreements. Neither had any relationship with the study sites.
- **Audio deletion:** Audio recordings were deleted after transcripts were verified and de-identified.
- **Retention:** De-identified transcripts, analytic files and the audit trail will be kept for five years after publication and then securely destroyed.

**S3.3 Ethical safeguards**

- Gatekeepers were not told who volunteered or took part. Researchers arranged scheduling directly with participants, booked rooms in non-clinical areas under a generic study title, and offered off-site or video interviews.
- Participants had at least 48 hours to consider taking part, gave written consent, and reconfirmed consent to recording at the start of each interview.
- Participants could withdraw their data without giving a reason until 14 days after interview.
- No combination of characteristics that could identify an individual is reported alongside any quotation.
- Participants could pause or stop the interview at any time, and information about the hospital employee-support service was available. No participant needed this support.
- If a participant disclosed an ongoing risk of serious harm to patients, the interviewer would encourage them to use organisational reporting channels. No such disclosure occurred.
